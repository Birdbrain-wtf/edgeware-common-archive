**What is a Dominant Assurance Contract?**

Assurance contracts are a product of game theory that seek to manage the “free rider problem.” The free rider problem describes a situation in which somebody might not personally contribute to the creation of a public good that would benefit the whole community, but could still reap the rewards of others’ contributions.

A simple assurance contract, also called a provision point mechanism, ensures that members of a group are bound to their contribution to a public good, as long as some contribution threshold is met (say, a specified number of contributors or amount of money). If the threshold isn’t met, all of the contributed money is refunded. This creates an incentive to contribute in the first place - either you get the public good, or you get your money back. It’s a win-win.

A Dominant Assurance Contract (DAC) is an assurance contract with a refund system built in to incentivize participation even more. In a Dominant Assurance Contract, the individual who offers the public good (the entrepreneur) pays the contributors a refund bonus if the contribution threshold is not met. If the threshold is met, the public good is created and the entrepreneur gets a bit of profit. This way if the contract is executed the entrepreneur is rewarded for the risk of creating such a contract, and if the contract fails to execute the contributors are rewarded a bonus for participating (Tabarrok).

The creator of DACs, Alex Tabarrok, believed that this contract set up would make participation in funding the public good the dominant choice for members of a community, as they would perceive a lack of risk and be excited by the potential for reward. The DAC theoretically creates a situation in which the contract would almost always gain the participation necessary to pass the contribution threshold.

**How do you decide how much to refund contributors?**

This is where it gets tricky.

While the DAC was created to further incentivize participation in the creation of a public good, it doesn’t take into account “preference revelation.” Essentially, the original DAC assumes homogeneous preferences and complete information (Tabarrok). But in the real world, different people have different ideas about the value of a public good, and an entrepreneur wouldn’t necessarily have information about those diverse preferences. So how can an entrepreneur use their knowledge of the existence of varied valuations to design a DAC?

One possibility is to create a varied refund program in which the amount returned should the contract fail to execute is decreased as more time or contributors are added. For example, for the first day or the first ten contributors, the refund might be $5, but for the next day or the next ten contributors, the refund could be just $4 (Quinn). This might work to increase early contribution and ensure successful execution of the project, but it may also decrease the probability that you reach the contribution threshold. It just depends on the contributor pool.

In order to choose the number, Harvard PhD candidate statistician Jameson Quinn suggests gathering data from a small subset of potential contributors, and then using that information to gauge design of the DAC for the general community. Meanwhile, Taborrok’s original paper suggests leveraging a situation in which a failed DAC reveals varied valuations of the public good to contributors.

**How has this actually played out?**

There have been many academic experiments testing the DAC concept, especially with attention to the preference revelation issue.

A 2017 paper by Timothy N.Cason and Robertas Zubrickas found that “the refund bonus rule need not be generous – the best results are obtained for the least generous refund bonus used, i.e., r = 0.25. Moreover, the mechanism with a low bonus rate performed equally well in a more complex environment with heterogeneous valuations, and reached a success rate of up to 80% in later periods.” This is despite the fact that “variance in total contributions is greater with heterogeneous than with homogeneous valuations in all refund bonus conditions” (Cason).

Moreover, a 2016 paper published by Zhi Li and his team considers two unique mechanisms for deciding these proportions:

“The uniform price auction mechanism (UPA) collects an endogenously determined uniform price from everyone offering at least that price, while the uniform price cap mechanism (UPC) collects the uniform price from everyone offering at least that price, plus the full offer of everyone offering less...UPC generates higher aggregate contributions than PR and PPM, leading to higher provision rates than PPM; UPA attracts much higher contributions, although it provides less frequently. This ranking emerges because high offers are more common (especially among high value people in the heterogeneous environment) in the uniform price mechanisms, where higher offers only increase the payment when needed for provision.” (Zhi et al.)

There is clearly a lot of thought work going into how to deal with diverse valuations of public goods in designing DACs. In his notable Quora experiment on the subject, Jameson Quinn further points out that public goods in the real world are not binary. He argues that in this context, setting a contribution goal can be limiting, as it discourages donations over that goal and doesn’t fit the fact that a lower donation amount could still go to some lesser version of the public good.

To respond to this issue, Quinn first suggests sequentially offering the chance to pledge while updating on current fundraising progress. That way, those who really value the good can contribute first, and the rest of the community will be more incentivized to pledge as they see the amount of contributions increasing and become more certain that the good can be funded. He also suggests offering “soft” assurance contracts. This would mean that the contract isn’t all or nothing. If you contribute and the contract doesn’t reach the threshold, you are still bound to contributing some amount - just not the full amount initially pledged. Instead, the contract has a softer margin. Quinn’s experiment implemented these ideas and was ultimately successful, with the last three donations for his chosen cause, the Center for Election Science, coming in the last thirty minutes.

**DACs in the Ecosystem of Blockchains**

It seems clear that there is a lot of potential for DACs to replace traditional means of funding public goods. It has even been suggested that DACs can replace most of the functions of modern government (Kuznicki). With the advent of Smart Contracts, experiments in implementing DACs for blockchains is bound to increase given the apparent ease of creating a DAC smart contract ([see the code for writing a Dominant Assurance Smart Contract here](https://programtheblockchain.com/posts/2018/05/01/writing-a-dominant-assurance-contract/)).

Amoveo, a Proof of Work based blockchain, offers an interesting case of using DACs to fund development of a blockchain-based network. Amoveo is currently executing DACs with three elements - a scope of work document, an oracle question, and a prediction mechanism. Their version of a DAC is arguably more powerful than an off-chain DAC, given that the development of the network would likely mean increased value of the currency (in addition to whatever other good the planned development offered). If the contract isn’t executed due to lack of contribution, the would-be free-riders who didn’t contribute are not left neutral, but are instead worse off, because they missed an opportunity to increase the value of their tokens.

Beyond straightforward DACs, there are certainly other entities in the blockchain space implementing crowdfunding for the public good with a refund element. Take the idea of a DAICO, for instance. This is a decentralized autonomous ICO that uses crowdfunding. If investors aren’t happy with the progress of developers, they can vote for a refund. Contributors send funds, get a network’s token, then funding is released over time according to votes by contributors (Pauw).

Giving contributors power over the release of funds and implementing votes to order refunds in this way makes the funding process not quite so simple as it is in the original DAC. It seems that in the case of a DAICO, a lot of excitement for the structure comes from the shifting of power towards contributors. This alludes to an idea that will be explored a bit more at the end of this article: the possibility of using tokens and voting to govern the use of public funds.

**Other Models for Incentivizing Funding the Public Good**

One tested method for funding a public good in a blockchain-based network is the use of a token bonding curve. A bonding curve is a mechanism of a curation market, invented by Simon de la Rouviere to facilitate coordination and accomplishment of group goals. Yos Riady gives a good summary of the mechanism: “the token bonding curve is an emerging cryptoeconomic primitive: protocol based incentive systems that enable coordination of network participants to achieve shared goals. Tokens incentivize players in an economic game towards an outcome that [is] mutually beneficial” (Riady).

A bonding curve might look exponential, saying that the token price increases as the supply of tokens increases. The curve may also take other shapes - some have explored the possibility of a linear equation, a rule-based function, and other variations (Lau).

Nexus Mutual is an insurance platform that uses an exponential bonding curve. The platform allows individuals to buy in to a mutual fund that they can then submit a claim to take money out of. The buy-in amount is determined according to how much would be needed to ensure everyone’s costs can be paid for at a given point in time and defined by the bonding curve. The buy-in amount is the Minimum Capital Requirement, a dynamic number that essentially says that as more people join the pool, more money is needed. With this set up, the Nexus Mutual token price increases as the amount of funds in the mutual increases, creating a “Continuous Token Model” (Karp).

There is no refund here like the one a DAC offers. Instead, by using the token bonding curve, as more people buy into the public good more of it is available. Similar to the token bonding curve in its exponential focus is the idea of Quadratic Voting (QV). In their paper proposing the concept of linking QV to crowdsourcing, Vitalik Buterin, Zoë Hitzig, and E. Glen Weyl argue for a version of de-centrally funding a public good that takes into account the preference revelation issue. They summarize their framework as follows:

“Individuals make public goods contributions to projects of value to them. The amount received by the project is (proportional to) the square of the sum of the square roots of contributions received. Under the “standard model” this yields first best public goods provision. Variations can limit the cost, help protect against collusion and aid coordination” (Buterin et al.)

The approach is called quadratic funding. It essentially says that projects with more donors, rather than total donation amount behind them, should be amplified - because they matter to more people. The concept is meant to shift entirely the worldview around what a public good is and how it might be delivered (and goes even further in redefining what a society could look like). Like the use of token bonding curves, the approach on surface-level focuses less on the game theory of how to incentivize participation and more on how to maximize contributions based on the value of the good.

The authors of the paper expect a great deal of experimentation around the concept. It will be curious to see the results - and whether there will be attempts to integrate or compare the game theory of DACs with the method.

**Are there regulations for DAC crowdfunding?**

The short answer is: probably, usually, yes. There aren’t clear rules outlined for the regulation of DACs. This is probably because they have not been widely implemented, and because they can be implemented in such diverse ways.

For the specific case of a public good that is created and governed by a blockchain protocol, the regulatory status is even more murky. What if the public good was a mechanism that served to further the development of a protocol and would make that protocol’s native currency more valuable (like Amovea’s model)? Is there precedence for that increase in value to be seen as profit, and therefore taxable? Moreover, while the spirit of the endeavor is clearly not for profit, seen through this murky water could tokens involved in the DAC be considered security tokens?

Given that the SEC just last week released new (and ambiguous) guidelines on the difference between and regulations for utility and security tokens, the regulatory status of a DAC that uses tokens for governance is a big question. But looking at the potential benefit this kind of contract could bring for funding the public good, it seems clearly worth exploring how DACs and the game theory at their foundation could be integrated more widely while complying with regulations.
