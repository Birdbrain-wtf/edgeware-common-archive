There has been a lot of buzz around Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs) in the last year. 

Any case in which people want greater ownership of the ways they live, work, and play could use a DAO to facilitate those activities. These cases often occur in the management of assets, facilitation of transactions, curation of products, and collaboration on work. 

Some specific areas that could benefit from DAO implementation in the future include:

- Philanthropy 
- Scientific funding
- Political groups
- News media 
- Social networks
- Real estate
- Housing markets 
- Sports 
- Casinos 
- Gaming 
- Insurance
- Research
- Supply chains
- Legal 
- Government 

There are innumerable possibilities for DAOs, both within and without these listed areas. **Here are some examples of current and potential types of DAOs.** 

*Philanthropic DAOs* 

Right now, when a donor gives money to a philanthropic cause, it usually travels through a long line of middlemen -  banks, lawyers, governments, and so on. Each of these steps takes away from the amount of money available to actually benefit the cause the donor originally sought to contribute to. 

With a charity DAO, these steps could be minimized and transparency could be maximized. A group already building a similar type of organization on the Ethereum platform is Humaniq. Their goal is to bring blockchain-based financial services to communities without access to banks (Davies). 

An important consideration when building a philanthropic DAO, however, is how to integrate ethics into the organization. If the community has full, unchecked control over where their funds are going, this might not always lead to the most socially responsible results. Using targeted governance strategies and some amount of planned centralization could solve that problem. 

*DAOs for Funding Environmentally Sustainable Development*

The idea of using blockchain technology to connect investors to sustainable development projects in already being explored by a few groups. One of these is Impactio, sponsored by the World Wildlife Foundation and ConsenSys. The project focuses on using the transparency of the blockchain to connect investors to projects that are genuinely making positive social and environmental change (Munro). 

Any number of DAOs could be spun up based on this goal, with smart contracts ensuring that both investors and projects are committed and clear on each other’s goals. 

Additionally, a DAO could be used to generate ideas about the best projects to fund or how specific projects could be optimized, according to the knowledge base funding them. 

*A DAO for the News?* 

News media is often described as a public good, even when the structure of news organizations does not support that labeling. Creating a news media DAO could allow for consumers to use their collective intelligence to support news sources and stories that they want to hear. It could also allow people to verify which stories are more or less true, based on their personal experiences (@joonian). 

Of course, a danger in creating a publicly driven news sources is that not all the information coming from the people would necessarily be correct. Moreover, it would be possible for strong biases to take over the news production process. 

For this kind of DAO it would be exceedingly important to set out the goals of the organization clearly and to integrate governance structures to avoid falling into the trap of producing news that is incorrect or influenced strongly by just a few voices. 

*Sports DAOs*

In the sports world, ideas for implementing DAOs revolve strongly around the fact that the DAO structure allows for crowdsourcing wisdom. 

Both in terms of in-game decisions and organizational choices, fans clearly have strong opinions, and creating a SportsDAO could allow sports groups to leverage the collective knowledge and feelings of their fanbase (Zurrer). Especially in an area that focuses on bringing a sense of camaraderie to the public, using an organizational system that gives participants greater opportunity to contribute meaningfully and make decisions together seems like a clear choice.  

How to design a sports DAO governance structure is a question that holds some creative potential. For instance, integrating a prediction market model into fan interactions could be a fun and imaginative use of the technology - but it could also take a lot of the fun out of a game. 

*Gaming DAOs*

The gaming industry is already stepping into the opportunities of DAO technology. MLB Champions by Lucid Sight, a digital collectibles game, is using smart contracts to give users more security and to create opportunities for players to do more with their product (Lucid Sight, Inc.). 

DAOs could also help on the development side. Kevin Chou, founder and CEO of gaming companies Kabam and Forte, has described the difficulty that free-to-play game developers experience when they have to choose between catering to the entire game-playing community, or to those who opt to pay for add-ons within the game (Chou). 

Chou believes that blockchain structure and crypto-economic incentives can be used to shift this dynamic. 

DAOs could certainly put the relationship between gamers and developers clearly in the hands of both parties. Using smart contracts to define how gaming organizations interact with users would allow the whole community to partake in decision making, instead of a small subset willing to pay for certain elements of a game. It would also give developers a stronger voice.  

*DAOs for Insurance Funds*  

An insurance DAO could make funding insurance claims much faster and pain-free compared to traditional insurance programs. 

It’s an idea that is already being tried by Nexus Mutual, a platform that allows individuals to buy in to a mutual fund that they can then submit a claim to take money out of. 

Nexus Mutual uses a token bonding curve to decide the buy-in amount. Then, when you need help, you submit a claim to the community. Participants can stake tokens to vote on claims, allowing the community to directly verify and support each other. 

*DAOs to Facilitate Smarter Supply Chains* 

Supply chain DAOs can be used not only to ensure efficiency and transparency in executing a transaction, but also to achieve targeted organizational goals. 

The University of Cambridge Institute for Sustainability Leadership recently tested this methodology. They connected major agricultural buyers with smallholder farmers through an exchange of information and benefits across a blockchain-based contract. In this instance, the buyers offered financial benefits for the sellers to provide social and ecological data on their sustainability practices (NeoSatoshi). 

A DAO could easily be created to serve exactly this purpose, and could be similarly applied across any supply chain with two parties seeking to make a secure and effective exchange. 

*Legal DAOs*

In May 2019, the first DAO to be legally registered as a blockchain-based LLC was created by dOrg (Weller). The purpose of this DAO is to support the legal compliance of future DAOs by offering legal guidelines, templates, and tools.

In the legal space, facilitating adherence to regulations is just one area that DAOs could be implemented. Aragon founder Luis Cuende spent some time at Web3 Summit 2019 arguing for a legal system that is made up of DAOs entirely (Web3 Foundation).

According to Cuende, the smart contracts of a DAO would make legal relationships much clearer and automatically enforced. However, he did acknowledge one important stipulation. 

Like philanthropic DAOs, legal DAOs would need some amount of regular human supervision. Smart contracts are not aware of the physical world, nor do they always represent the actual intent of the parties creating them (because, after all, they are just code made by simply humans). 

For these reasons, a legal DAO would have to be carefully designed and governed to ensure proper navigation of sticky or evolving legal situations.   

**A Few Caveats**

These are only a few concrete examples of the benefits DAOs could bring to spaces that are currently dominated by traditional coordination structures. 

Any space where people want more autonomy, transparency, and security in their organization could benefit from implementing a DAO. 

That said, many of the use cases suggested above need a lot more research to implement well. Some areas that DAOs are experimenting on now is how much development to engage in - as much as fast as possible, or only when necessary? Another big question is how much centralization, and in what forms, is okay in the development of a DAO.

The answers to both of those points of conflict ultimately lie in the specific community and purpose of each DAO. The more we experiment with using DAO technology to optimize the organizations we participate in every day, the more we’ll know how to respond to all of these challenges. 

An important consideration in forming DAOs to work for more traditional industries is the fact that putting an organization fully in the hands of its participants can be slow and occasionally hard to navigate. 

This problem leads to another point of discord in the DAO development community: whether to spur as much participation as possible, or to use the amorphous structure of DAOs to allow for varying levels and mechanisms for participation. 

Many governance mechanisms have been suggested to test what varying types of participation might look like. One, liquid democracy, allows for users to delegate their voting allowance to others who serve as experts. Meanwhile, another method called holographic consensus resembles governance with an electoral college, with one group of voting experts acting as such. 

There are still other novel governance ideas that could be implemented to suit the needs of a DAO. One example is reputation-based voting, where organizations give more control over decision-making to those who have had success (which could be defined differently depending on the group) in the past. 

Another governance option that a DAO might explore is a futarchy. In this system, people vote on values instead of policies. Then, for each proposal, a prediction markets judges how well the suggestion meets the community values. This metric is used to decide whether or not to implement the policy. 

**The Takeaway**. Different DAO structures, governance styles, and crypto-economic incentives will work best for different use cases. Over the long term, it could even be possible to use prediction tools to help DAOs with different goals make decisions about these coordination elements. The exciting opportunity of a platform like Commonwealth.im is the potential for different communities to create DAOs that operate how they feel best, and to experiment with what that looks like.
