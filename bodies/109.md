### Quote of the Day
 
**"Can we replace Argentina with a DAO?"** *- Santiago Siri*

## The Recap

Day 1 of Web3 Summit 2019 featured a wide range of talks that continually returned to focus on the exciting and unclear future for human networks using blockchain technology. More than anything, sentiments revolved around building intentionally and using new technologies to create opportunities for exploring untapped human potential. 

Captured here are talks from 

- Luis Cuende of Aragon One,
- Dominic Tarr of Secure Scuttlebutt, 
- Hashmap from Grin, 
- Jutta Steiner and Shawn Tabrizi of Parity Technologies,
- Ryan Zurrer of Web3 Foundation, 
- and Michael Zargham of BlockScience,
- ..as well as panels on Ethereum 2.0 and DAOs. 

### Founding Technology That Considers Future Generations

The first main stage talk of the day, given by CEO of Aragon One and co-founder of Aragon Luis Cuende, began with a powerful image: Cuende’s hope for a future where his children could enjoy the same mountains and lakes he did in his childhood. To create this future, he believes it is important to shift from a short-term focus on profit to long-term alignment on goals. In Cuende’s words,

"We are trading a lot of these long-term opportunities that we have on earth, which is...the only planet we know which has life, and also probably one of the most beautiful ones out there...for short-term benefits for shareholders." 

In order to build this wide perspective into actions, Cuende argued for the importance of setting out a manifesto at the very beginning of an organization’s formation. This manifesto should include a list of values and principles ranked by priority for the community. Ideally, in times of conflict this list would be inclusive enough to help sort out any dispute. 

**A Principled, DAO-based System of Law**  

Cuende’s talk went on to explore expanding this idea of manifesto-based organizations to a DAO-based system of law. He began by identifying three main challenges in DAO design: 

DAOs are made of “dry” code. Basically, DAOs are formed of computer code designed to execute some action based on some parameters. This makes them automatically enforceable, but it also makes them inflexible.
DAOs are self-referential - the contracts that make them up have no way to know what’s happening in the physical world, the “meatspace.” 
The actions executed in a DAO may not match the original human intent, due to a bug or poor coding or other unforeseen consequences - because behind the set-up of the DAO, are simply humans. 

On the opposite end of dry code, Cuende described “wet code,” which is the sort of code that exists in real world contracts written in human languages This sort of code has its own drawbacks, as it is often much easier to dispute and can create uncertainty. Meanwhile, the enforcers of wet code usually make these contracts slow, expensive, and difficult to navigate. 

By mixing dry and wet code, Cuende believes a decentralized system of law could be established. This rule of law would be community governed and secured by crypto-economic incentives.

Of course, right now DAO technology and governance are very experimental. However, Cuende set out a vision for the future where organizations that are software-based could rely on this sort of rule system to govern all interactions, so long as the system of law included a conflict-resolution system robust and comprehensive enough to manage all disputes. 

Cuende even pulled in his own personal experience of a bad interaction with a landlord in order to assert that legal cases handled through a DAO save time, money, and the heart-ache that all too commonly occurs in current legal disputes. Here, Cuende circled back to the importance of a manifesto in founding a decentralized legal system. 

**Intentional Building with Secure Scuttlebutt and Grin** 

Principled approaches and sustainable development in the blockchain technology space was a celebrated theme for the rest of the day at Web3 Summit. Even talks introducing very specific technologies included that long-view future focus. Dominic Tarr gave a talk on Secure Scuttlebut, a PoS database that he dramatically introduced as prepared for a zombie apocalypse - because if you’re prepared for that, then you’re prepared for anything. 

Scuttlebutt has thousands of blockchains with one staker per chain, in an infrastructure designed to allow Peer-to-Peer interactions to verify the contents of a chain. In this infrastructure, there is no full node, but rather each user replicates the feeds that are relevant to themselves, with all the data held offline.    

While the goal of the talk was to introduce Secure Scuttlebutt, the highlight was Tarr’s focus on building useful tools for the future. Tarr introduced a development philosophy of creating something that is useful even from its beginning, but also described the importance of technology that has long-term use value. It can be difficult to separate hype from a clear view of sustainability, Tarr contended as he showed a graph of ETH price over time. But Tarr concluded that in the end, the truly utilitarian technologies win out, echoing Cuende’s emphasis on shifting away from products designed just to maximize short-term profit. 

Another theme was the push and pull between speedy, constant development and slow-moving, restrained development. Hashmap, a core developer at privacy-focused cryptocurrency Grin, explained why his team embraces the mindset of Dieter Rams: Good design is as little design as possible. At the base of this idea for Grin is the echoing focus on future generations. In Grin’s case, this means focusing on protecting universal access, privacy, and freedom for the future. 

Hashmap used the analogy of a tree - Grin is growing slowly, but will be there for your grandchildren. The talk explained a few previous development steps and why they were rolled back, and truly emphasized Grin’s attempt to seek fairness and utility for the community. Hashmap described it as a social experiment, with governance conducted by a small team that does not encourage people to use it, but rather wants only those who naturally arrive and see utility in using the coin. 

### Substrate and Polkadot: Tools for Building a Better Future 

Two talks of the day focused on Substrate and Polkadot, and the intention of creating smart development opportunity behind these tools. 

Shawn Tabrizi of Parity Technologies explained the utility of Substrate. He opened the discussion by first iterating why the future will have blockchains - in short, to have less trust and more truth. 

Tabrizi went on to describe Substrate’s value as the framework for building that future. He described their grounding philosophies for the project to be extensible, modular, open source. This introduction indicates Parity’s perception of the current emphasis of founding development on clear principles.  

The talk also included a brief explainer on Parity’s different visions for the future of Polkadot and Substrate. Polkadot is built on Substrate, but Parity imagines a future where there will be other solo and parachains also built with Substrate. Polkadot is also an open protocol - you don’t need to build on Substrate to use Polkadot. 

Tabrizi concluded by elucidating how Substrate hopes to be the best framework for building the future decentralized web. He emphasized a few points:

- Substrate is free, and always will be. 
- There are a huge amount of contributors, a growing trend that will be encouraged to continue. 
- There is a focus on building a community of technical developers, with an open chat with direct access to Parity engineers, as well as community meet ups. There is a hope that these meet ups don’t just facilitate business meetings, but also allow for making new friends!

The talk concluded with stress on Substrate’s purpose as a tool for building a better future, with Tabrizi shouting out current builders like Polkascan and Centrifuge who are already working with Substrate towards this goal.

Ken Seiff’s AMA with Parity Founder and CEO Jutta Steiner also gave attention to the foundational ideas and future hopes for Parity and the Polkadot project. 

Steiner recounted her experience founding Parity as stemming from excitement around building better institutions, as well as using math and technology to solve human coordination problems. Like Tabrizi’s description of Substrate, Steiner emphasized Polkadot’s role in giving people tools. However, Steiner detailed specifically Polkadot’s significance for accomplishing the goal of better and more free organization, as well as the importance of on-chain governance in achieving that future. 

### Ethereum 2.0 and DAOs: Visions for Leveraging New Technologies

Day 1 also featured two panels: One on Ethereum 2.0, and one on DAOs. The Ethereum 2.0 panel featured Mamy Ratsimbazafy of Status, Wei Tang of Parity Technologies, and Adrian Manning of Sigma Prime, with Rick Dudley of Vulcanize moderating. 

The main goal of the introduction of Ethereum 2.0 is to move from a Proof-of-Work network to Proof-of-Stake and increase scalability. Throughout the discussion, the conservative nature of this shift was emphasized. There will be a phased approach, with Phase 0 including deployment of a new blockchain beacon chain, Phase 1 introducing shards of data, and Phase 2 comprising layering execution semantics on top of those shards. Ethereum 2.0 will be a modular networking stack. 

Much of the talk emphasized the specifics of how Ethereum 2.0 will be built. The group rounded out the discussion by emphasizing that it’s still very early and there are a lot of unknowns around what people will want, as well as how to achieve what makes the most sense to offer. 

The second panel, on DAOs, also focused on the challenge of creating something that works for a community. However, this discussion featured many more diverse tactics for figuring out what that something might look like. 

The panel featured Santiago Siri, founder of Democracy Earth Foundation, Luis Cuende of Aragon, Mariano Conti of MakerDAO, Cassandra Shi of Moloch DAO, and Shaun Djie of Digix. The beginning of the discussion showcased the differences in focus, governance goals, and challenges for each of the DAOs that the panel members work on. 

A common theme was the question of how to handle participation and community coordination. There was a clear disconnect elucidated between wanting people who have stake in the organization to have a louder voice, and the freedom of seeing who naturally takes part in governance. A big part of this is the issue of how to handle identity and anonymity. 

The conversation then shifted to asking just how far DAOs can be scaled. This is where we got our quote of the day, with Santiago Siri asking, "Can we replace Argentina with a DAO?" The resounding answer was yes - but first, we should start a lot smaller. 

From a technical perspective, the panel believed it would be possible to replace a national government with a DAO.  From a more goal-oriented perspective, they also explained that implementing DAO-based governance would clearly benefit the people of any country with an unstable currency and a traditional government with too much control. 

However, how to handle identity and other issues, like vote buying and manipulation, is still very uncertain - hence the need to start small and with a lot of experimentation. In order to start those baby experimental steps towards good DAO governance, Cassandra Shi brought up the necessity of a space for DAO participants to get easy access to the most relevant information for decision-making. 

The group wrapped up the DAO discussion by emphasizing the opportunity to make human organizations much more efficient presented by DAOs. However, Mariano Conti concluded by stressing the difference between traditional governance and DAO governance. In particular, Conti argued that there should be lots of experimentation with DAO governance, and a separate evolution of governance structures that after evolution is applied to traditional systems. 

### How to Guide the Evolution of Blockchain Networks

Talks by Ryan Zurrer and Michael Zargham focused a lot more explicitly on the foundational ideas and long-term trends behind the kinds of networks being built in the ecosystem today. 

Zurrer focused specifically on the history of DAOs and where they might be going. To Zurrer, DAOs are exciting for their potential to generate both human and financial capital, as well as offering mission-driven groups a chance to escape the traditional divide of for-profit or for charity. 

A key benefit of the amorphous structure of DAOs, in Zurrer’s mind, is that it allows for different levels of participation. Zurrer sees this as a definite positive that DAOs should be designed to take advantage of. 

Zurrer also emphasized being pragmatic about decentralization. While decentralization is obviously the end goal for a DAO, during the actual creation of organizations Zurrer believes it can sometimes be useful to have some amount of centralization for at least some time period. 

In his mind, MakerDAO is an example of a group that requires over-participation. Zurrer supports instead a model where those interested can vote on what they are passionate about, and day-to-day operations and other more basic votes can be pushed down to functional teams.

Rounding out his argument for varied levels of participation, Zurrer argued that low voter turn-out should not be a focus for development teams. Instead, emphasis should be on the fact that such a small subset of a community can arrive at coherent decisions. In his mind, the wider community should be seen as more of a security feature. Curating information for this community is a huge part of the success of this model, which echoes Cassandra Shi’s call for ease of access to relevant information for voters in a DAO. 

As for the future of DAOs, Zurrer asserted that core teams will be a much bigger driver of success than are massive decentralized communities. On the other hand, however, he celebrated the due diligence paid by the community of The DAO, and argued that if it hadn’t fallen apart it would have been the largest capital deployer in the ecosystem. 

Zurrer also expressed excitement over the potential for liquid democracy, and the possibility of reputation-weighted peer review. This latter concept would allow for different teams with different metrics to measure and reward members of the core team more accurately, in order to build talented teams that drive DAO success. He also sees potential in stablecoins, unique financial tools in cryptoeconomics, and the introduction of mechanisms for collateralization. 

The conclusion of the talk featured a renewed focus on principle, with Zurrer urging the community to proceed in a “noble and right” manner. The next talk, given by Michael Zargham, provided a framework for thinking about how these sorts of principles can be used to intentionally craft a human infrastructure that offers the best opportunities for the complex networks that new technologies create. 

Zargham’s talk used cybernetics to explore the social and economic structures brought about by Web 3.0. 

Cybernetics is the science of steering systems, with an awareness of interacting systems co-steering each other. According to Zargham, this science can be used to estimate how we affect the decentralized systems we’re building, and how the systems affect us. 

An important difference to grasp is the divide between infrastructure and products. In this case, the products are the various blockchain technologies that are evolving, while the infrastructure is the invisible layer that allows people to use these technologies. Importantly, we, the people using these technologies cannot be separated from this infrastructure. Instead, it should be viewed as a complex adaptive system, with feedback loops created from our interaction with it. 

There are many challenges to understanding these feedback loops, and with the infrastructural leaps and bounds provided by the Web 3.0, there is a need to get more information, make models, and create tools to better understand and leverage those feedback loops. 

Zargham seemed to respond to Mariano Conti’s point, that DAO governance should evolve separately from traditional governance structures, by iterating the need to look at the tools we use to design traditional, centralized infrastructure systems to inform how we think about building steering mechanisms for decentralized systems. 

Another significant caveat Zargham mentioned is that these steering mechanisms should be embedded into the complex systems in a way that does not tell the users what to do, but rather gives us opportunities to learn about how we can adapt the system to leverage the potential for human infrastructure in the evolving tech. He also concluded that informed participants will be able to better craft these systems, repeating a sentiment from earlier talks on the power of relevant knowledge.  

Overall, like in many of the talks, Zargham emphasized being informed and purposeful with how we create the Web 3.0 future. His talk brought the conversation back to Cuende’s point from the beginning of the day: that we must have alignment on clear goals for how these systems should evolve.

The talks covered here are not the only ones that happened on Day 1 at the Web3 summit, nor are talks representative of everything that happens at the conference. This is especially true in the context of Hackerspaces, spaces designed specifically for discussion on any topic at all that might draw some feedback from people in the community.
