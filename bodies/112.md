### Assume a Spherical Cow

Or, instead, assume a crypto-Twitter. That’s the future platform angel investor Balaji Srinivasan envisioned when explaining how pseudonymous identities could be linked to reputation, without losing anonymity. 

In the world we live in now, even when an online identity is pseudonymous for the purpose of being anonymous, there are ways to track activity and see patterns in a way that makes it possible to clearly identify the person behind the pseudonym. This becomes a problem when you need to be truly anonymous, but you also need to interact with others online in a way that lets you use the weight of your reputation. 

This is where Srinivasan’s future crypto-Twitter comes in. In this example, reputation is quantified in followers. Obviously, if you created a pseudonym and transferred all of your public followers to it, it wouldn’t be very anonymous. Yet, you need followers to give you reputational weight. 

Srinivasan’s answer to this issue is twofold. First, the crypto-Twitter would need a back end that would allow for a zero-knowledge transfer of followers. A zero-knowledge (ZK) proof is a tool that allows you to prove that some information is true, without revealing the contents of that information. In this example, the ZK proof would conclusively tell the Twitter operator that you have, say, 1,000 followers, but it wouldn’t reveal any information about who those followers are. In this way, the system could verify your pseudonym, proving that you have a reputation without ever knowing the details behind that reputation. 

While this verification shouldn’t reveal the pseudonym’s true identity, it still reveals some information. Thus comes the second part of Srinivasan’s solution: an “auto-follow” system. The auto-follow would be a feature that allows accounts to automatically follow verified accounts (this could theoretically be refined to have parameters around what sort of verified accounts are followed, but for this example, Srinivasan kept it simple). 

The auto-follow would serve to create noise in the data. Anyone trying to trace the real identity of a pseudonym, based on the information they post, amount of followers, or other data, would be unable to rely on any information about followers to trace the identity. 

The idea is similar to one David Chaum introduced at Web3 Summit 2019. Chaum’s vision is to use decoy votes to end vote-buying. The idea is that anyone can take a decoy voting ballot, but the knowledge that it is a decoy would only be known to the one who took the ballot. This would mean that anyone trying to buy a vote would have no way to know whether the ballot they were sold is a decoy or real, driving down the incentive to buy votes. Harry Halpin introduced another closely-related concept with NYM technologies - the idea of creating decoy traffic in order to confuse anyone looking for patterns in user data (Web3 Foundation). 

**A reputation bridge is a safe way to link a reputation to a pseudonymous identity without giving up anonymity.** By quantifying reputation and connecting it to a pseudonym, anonymous identities can be verified and given credibility, which could help minimize malicious acts by telling people who they can and can’t trust in a way that doesn’t sacrifice privacy. This is an innovation that would not just be useful in the context of a crypto-Twitter, but on any voting platform. 

### Application

While the crypto-Twitter platform is necessary for quantifying reputation with followers, there could feasibly be other ways to quantify reputation for different kinds of platforms. For instance, if it was a voting platform, we could use the number of votes given to the successful majority that a participant has in their record to quantify reputation - and then use a similar process to show proof of reputation without revealing information about the votes themselves. 

What are other ways reputation could be quantified? Where else might we want to connect a reputation bridge to a pseudonymous identity?
