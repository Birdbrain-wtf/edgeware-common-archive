### What is an oracle in the blockchain world?

Oracles are the truth-giving tools that send data to and from smart contracts to allow for proper execution. For instance, imagine Alice participates in a smart contract that says that Bob will send a certain amount of money to her if she sends Bob an email. The smart contract would have all the information necessary to send Alice the money once the email is received by Bob. But once the contract is deployed, it can’t be edited and has no way to find information about whether or not Bob received his present. 

This is where an oracle comes in. The oracle’s job is to find out whether or not Bob actually received the email. As soon as the oracle confirms that Bob received Alice’s email, it sends that information to the smart contract. The smart contract is assured that the parameters were filled, and it executes the final task of sending Alice her money. 

Oracles can either be software or hardware oracles, and they can be inbound or outbound. The names are pretty indicative of their qualities, but with technology as new and significant as oracles, we might as well start with some definitions. Software oracles are meant to collect information available online, while hardware oracles collect data from hardware located and collecting info in the physical world. Meanwhile, inbound oracles provide external information to the smart contract and outbound oracles allow smart contracts to send information out (Voshmgir).

### What are the challenges for developing oracles?

There are three ways to structure an oracle:
- Integrated within a protocol, coded by and for that particular chain.
- Centralized, using a trusted third party to supply information.
- Decentralized, using a network of oracle nodes.

The main argument against an integrated oracle is that it is inefficient in terms of both time and cost (Chainlink). Moreover, a centralized oracle has all the drawbacks that anything centralized does: it requires a lot of trust in a third party, which can be dangerous in the case of technologies so important for the execution of smart contracts. 

That leaves the option of decentralized oracles. Even with this option, though, there are some major challenges to implementing a network of oracle nodes. In his overview of decentralized oracles, Julien Thevenard of Fabric Ventures describes several potential issues for these oracle models. Perhaps the clearest risk is that of a majority attack, in which a significant number of nodes are owned by the same entity, creating the potential for one person to sway the perceived data towards a self-serving falsehood.  

Another danger is that a node might collect data once, then share it off-chain with other nodes it might control to reduce operating costs - not a big deal if the information they collected that one time is correct, but a serious issue if the information is incorrect, especially since an error correction security measures might not pick up on this. Freeloading, the willful and malicious copying of another oracle’s data without verification, is another issue. Data corruption and the possibility of the data supplied by the oracles revealing sensitive information about the query are even more challenges faced by oracles (Thevenard). 

Thevenard also introduces three possible ways to mitigate these issues:

- Multiple data sources
- Staking requirements for participation in oracle
- Trusted execution environment (TEEs) 

These are all options slowly being explored, but the importance of experimenting and finding an ideal oracle model is imminently important. This fact was recently showcased by questions regarding concerns about MakerDAO’s oracle. 

### Questions about MakerDAO’s oracle?

MakerDAO’s oracle uses a geographically distributed team of nodes that are all owned by people who hold MKR. Their stake in the DAO creates an incentive to provide good information. Each oracle provides a price feed, with the controlling addresses made public. There are 24, but they all are not always active. The same weighted price sources are used by each of the oracles. The price feed goes through a smart contract called the “medianizer” that ensures that any feed that differs from the majority is not counted (robika001). 

It sounds like a good system. Yet [when the Maker DAI Bot posted a Tweet](https://mobile.twitter.com/MakerDaiBot/status/1176586149948796928) that cited the Ether price at the time CDP #16619 got liquidated as $157.58/ETH, people asked questions -- because this was far lower than the value listed on commonly referenced exchange resources.

Maybe something went wrong with MakerDAO’s oracle system, or maybe it didn’t. Either way, it is clear that people want more clarity on how oracles are functioning, and assurance that this crucial technology is actually implemented effectively. 

One response to how price feeds could be better facilitated came in the form of the BitDex white paper. Instead of pushing oracles onto users, this decentralized finance platform introduces a financial contract in which players bring their own price, and other participants can pull a request for an oracle service if they want to dispute that price. The team behind the tech believes that the threat of dispute will keep users honest, minimizing the need for oracles at all (0xlildex). 

The BitDex method is not just a solution for price feed oracles, but rather could serve as a broader dispute mitigation tool. As IBM’s Krishna Ratakonda has stated, “Blockchain acts as a common source of truth, processing, organizing and connecting data in a way that was not possible before” (Queen). As smart contracts continue to prove their value both in decentralized finance and far beyond, the structure of oracles will only become more important as the technology providing that source of truth.
