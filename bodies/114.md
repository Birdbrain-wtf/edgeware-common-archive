Ideas about the implications of quantum computing for cryptography have been circulating for a while, with opinions generally ranging from “it will have no effect” to “we’re doomed.” Regardless of your stance, it’s important to at least consider a future in which this technology will significantly alter our infrastructure. 

### The Rapid Development of Quantum Processing Power

In order to build an understanding of what quantum computers will mean for the world of blockchain, we’ll start with some basic definitions. Right now, everyday computers use bits - binary pieces of computing power that scale linearly. The pieces of computing power used by quantum computers, qubits, are able to represent both zeros and ones at once and thus scale exponentially. This means that every qubit added to a system doubles its computation power. 

In 2015, computers were developed that used 4 qubits. By 2018, Intel had created a 49-qubit processor, and Google had made a 72-qubit system (Matier). Just yesterday, Google officially announced that its quantum machine achieved “quantum supremacy,” meaning that their computer was able to complete a calculation that even the best classical computer could not complete (at least not within a time frame of fewer than 10,000 years) (Arute et al.). 

Quantum computers are clearly developing at a very fast pace. Will the extraordinary power of quantum computers mean the end of cryptographic security? 

### Quantum Implications for Blockchain

The extreme power behind quantum computers has obvious implications for blockchains that use Proof-of-Work. Because miners using classical computers are the ones securing the chain, the introduction of a more powerful computer could be detrimental to the safety of a PoW network. For example, there has been speculation that the Grover algorithm, which allows for quadratic speed up in searches, could be used to break a hashing function like SHA-256 (this is the technology Bitcoin is based on). Previous analysis has shown that right now, this would still not be a profitable task for the quantum hacker, but it is still a strong possibility that faster algorithms could enhance the speed up and make this a possible attack vector (Tessler). 

The encryption of private keys with AES is another point of concern for similar reasons. However, these are fairly easy issues to resolve by simply making your AES-protected key longer. For example, while a 128-bit Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) key could be broken by a quantum computer, economic and time constraints mean that an AES-256 key should be safe (Sedgwick). 

The main threat that doesn’t have a very simple solution is the potential for Shor’s algorithm to break a public key protected using the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA). Shor’s algorithm, a quantum integer factorization algorithm, can be used to derive a private key from a public one and empty a wallet. While the answer here isn’t quite as simple as “make a longer key,” projects like the Quantum Resistant Ledger (QRL) have already shown that the use of hash and lattice-based cryptography can mitigate these risks (Matier). 

Again, the above concerns are most clearly relevant for Proof of Work chains. There is a strong sentiment that quantum computers should not affect the cryptography of Proof of Stake chains. Because a node’s ability to verify a block relies on its stake, not its computing power, it is widely seen as a much less likely risk (QRL cites this as their reason for using Proof of Stake) (Dalton). However, this is a sentiment against which many have already expressed caution. 

Especially with technology as relatively new and isolated as quantum computing, it is of key importance to take security based on classical computing power with a grain of salt. As the exploration below suggests, quantum technology does not just mean faster computers - it could implicate a whole new technology infrastructure. 

### A Quantum Coin?

Another completely different way of looking at the problem explores the possibility of creating a Qubit coin: a cryptocurrency based on quantum computing. One approach to creating a quantum coin focuses on quantum computing’s no-cloning theorem. Building a system based on this theorem would make it impossible to clone and distribute a blockchain ledger of qubits. Qubit coins could act more as actual physical coins, with no ability to “double-spend” them or otherwise lie about their data. Transactions would be local and immediate, without a need for active Internet access (Jogenfors). 

This type of cryptocurrency chain could be implemented because of the property of quantum entanglement. Quantum entanglement describes the phenomenon in which a quantum particle, when generated, cannot be defined independently from all other quantum particles. Essentially, the state of the particle is defined by all other particles equally. Thus, you could have a Qubit coin transaction without needing to engage in Proof of Work or Proof of Stake - the creation of a coin would verify the existence of the rest of the chain and its place in it by very nature (Tessler). This is also where the no-cloning element comes from.

The sort of entanglement described in the above method is spatial entanglement, in which each coin is definitively linked to each other coin across space. What happens to one coin happens to every other coin it is attached to. Quantum entanglement in time has been used to propose another method for creating a qubit coin. 

The concept of entanglement in time basically describes a system in which each quantum particle is inextricably linked to all past and future particles, with changes to one radiating through time. For a blockchain, this would mean that each part of the ledger besides the current bit is viewed as no longer in existence, but all qubit coins are readable because of their immutable tie to all the qubit coins before them. The researchers behind the idea actually describe the design as creating a “quantum networked time machine” (Rajan). 

There is one clear note in the original paper introducing this sort of quantum coin that shows the benefit that could come from quantum researchers teaming up with leading experts in the blockchain consensus space. The authors explain that, given the fact that many well-respected classical blockchains don't have Byzantine fault tolerance, "it seems unreasonable to demand full security proofs for Byzantine fault tolerance in our quantum design" (Rajan). It’s a fair point, and the researchers do iterate that the paper is not meant to lay out a fully thought out quantum blockchain structure and governance model. However, it does demonstrate the room for growth behind the current thoughts circulating on building a quantum blockchain. 

### Application

*“In a future where quantum technologies are commonplace it will make the most sense to re-invent the blockchain and transaction chain with the methods of quantum cryptography”*(Tessler).    

Of course, this perspective assumes that quantum technology does indeed become commonplace. There is always the possibility that quantum technology will receive the same treatment as nuclear fission, remaining basically untouched (Sedgwick). But the strong forward march of blockchain technology, even without a remarkably strong and sellable “killer app,” suggests that this could be a good space for innovative quantum technology to slowly incubate.
