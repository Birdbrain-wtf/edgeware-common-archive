Dr. Gavin Wood recently explained why he thinks we are in the last phases of a so-called “Golden Age of Blockchain.” In his view, open-source development has been celebrated in the space for a long time, but we’re about to see the end of that. According to Dr. Wood, with increased competition, collaboration and openness will be replaced by competitive attitudes and profit-seeking motivations. 

Despite this pessimistic tone, there are still many who are working to encourage, sustain, and scale opportunities for open-source software. Dries Buytaert, one of the minds behind Drupal, has outlined some clear challenges and possibilities he sees for scaling open source projects.

### Recommendations for Sustaining Open-Source

Buytaert defines people engaged with open source projects as either Makers, those who develop out of and contribute back to open-source software, or Takers, those who monetize open source projects without giving back. Obviously, a significant part of scalability is sustainability. Open-source software will not be continually developed if end users are mostly Takers. 

In understanding how to solve this problem, Buytaert first explains the separation of common and public goods. Common goods are defined as rivalrous, or those that diminish with use, while public goods are non-rivalrous, a never-ending resource. Open-source software is a public good, but for companies that answer to corporate sponsors, customers are a common good. Therefore, it is necessary to develop governance mechanisms for open source software that encourage software free-riders but discourages customer free-riders (Buytaert).  

Buytaert describes the three ways this problem is usually dealt with as self-governance, centralization (central maintenance) or privatization (maintenance in exchange for exclusive benefits). 

According to Buytaert, all open source projects, whether they rely on self-governance, privatization, or centralization to manage collaboration, need to set straightforward standards around membership, appropriation rights, and contribution duties. To implement this sort of system, Buytaert argues for the importance of monitoring, rewards, and sanctions. 

Buytaert offers several recommendations for sustaining open source projects, including:

- Appeal to community self-interest by explaining the benefits of open-source
- Encourage end users to offer selective benefits to Makers (ie, encourage these end-users to mandate contribution back to Makers in some way)
- Use licenses to moderate how much access users have, or otherwise build in fairness principles 

Another important note Buytaert makes is that governance models are one thing, but it is also important to introduce novel methods of including underrepresented groups and improving inclusiveness. 

### Gitcoin: Using Cryptocurrency to Support Open Development

A good example of a project that plays well with Buytaert’s suggestions is Gitcoin. The platform gives exposure to open source projects who offer bounties for developers to do work on their software. There is no token associated with the platform. Instead, Gitcoin supports all ERC20 tokens (Singh). 

According to Kevin Owocki, one of the founders of Gitcoin, 96% of applications use open-source software [Ethereal Summit]. Given how much of the world depends on free code, it is crucial to find ways to support efforts towards collaboration and openness. Owocki believes the blockchain ecosystem is a perfect space for such an effort, because of the strong need for developers and the high market cap. 

Gitcoin lets people avoid the sort of HR process traditional companies use and instead match complementary projects and developers. In creating this sort of opportunity for symbiosis, Gitcoin creates a more sustainable way for open-source projects to develop.

By matching developers with appropriate open source projects, there are clear benefits for those who are invested in the development of the open-source software and those who claim the bounty. This system responds well to Buytaert’s appeal for end-users to support contribution to the software. Moreover, the success of the platform shows the importance of integrating incentives into the development process.

To tackle the problem of finding funding in the first place, Owocki has previously laid out quite a few possibilities. The most obvious choice would be the patronage of a benevolent billionaire, though Owocki notes that this method could make projects prone to capture by partial interested. Owocki has also noted the importance of finding better ways to value the work developers do (an abstract goal by nature) and applying that knowledge to developer rewards [Ethereal Summit]. 

One option for encouraging crowdsourcing could be another tactic that Gitcoin uses to facilitate open-source development: a grants program that uses Glenn E. Weyl’s Capital-constrained Liberal Radicalism (CLR) funding method (we’ve also called this quadratic funding). 

### How Else Can We Encourage Open-source Funding?

Beyond Owocki’s suggestions, there are quite a few fundraising methods work based on game theory that could be used in driving sustainable open-source funding.

We’ve previously explored [crowdfunding methods with Dominance Assurance Contracts, DAICOs, and Quadratic Funding.](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/101) Here, we’ll look at an interactive coin offering, optimal coin offering, and a “call-out” assurance contract. The idea is that these ideas could be used in end user communities to support open source software. 

*Interactive Coin Offering*

An interactive coin offering designs a token offering mechanism by which buyers offer personal valuation information in order for the coin valuation to reach a fair market equilibrium. Potential buyers either enter or exit the crowdsale each block epoch. When they enter, the buyer specifies the maximum sale valuation that they, personally, are comfortable with given their specified desired purchase amount. if the valuation ever exceeds this number their funds are automatically withdrawn (Teutsch). 

*Optimal Coin Offering*

The goal of an optimal coin offering is to get at the buyer’s most truthful valuation. The introduction to the paper describes it as:

“"a truthful multi-unit Vickrey-Dutch auction of callable tokens (i.e., a new hybrid security of tokens packaged with callable warrants). Truthful bidding is an ex-post Nash equilibrium strategy and the auction terminates with an ex-post efficient allocation; additionally, the callability of the warrants eliminates the winner’s curse of the auction and its underpricing" (Sánchez).

In simple terms, the coin offering is held as an auction, which ends with the highest bidder winning, but paying at the price the second-highest bidder offered. This is a Vickrey-Dutch auction: where the auction prices are descending, but the bidders offer more honest valuations because they want to offer the highest price in order to win, but don’t want to offer too much to avoid overpaying (Mishra). Meanwhile, the tokens themselves act as callable warrants, allowing bidders to receive a share of the company at a specific price in the future.  

*Call-out Assurance Contracts*

Basically that participants accept a system where they are systematically asked the minimum amount of money % they would donate to a thread of existing charities, and the charity that has the highest minimum % has that amount % donated by each player. The goal is to leverage the amount of money given to popular projects -- you can opt out by saying 0, but you can also get the whole community behind yours by giving a very high percent - keep N small (number of random accounts selected) to avoid a Sybil attack (vbuterin). 

## Application 

It could be interesting to apply any of these mechanisms to an investor group for open source projects. These game theory-based funding strategies could be implemented by end-users who all buy into a specific group of open-source software projects, ensuring that users are locked into giving back to open source projects in a fair way. Especially if we truly are heading into a period of increased competition and decreased collaboration, it will be more and more important to integrate incentives and game theory into open source funding plans.
