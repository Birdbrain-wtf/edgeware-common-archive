### What is OTC trading?

Over-the-Counter (OTC) trading is trading of securities that do not appear on a formal securities exchange. While exchanges are limited in liquidity to the individuals participating in the market and the liquidity of each currency involved at any point in time, OTC markets are meant to present an opportunity for more liquidity, as well as better communication and privacy (Dale). Closely related to the term OTC is the idea of a Decentralized Exchange (DEX), an exchange for which there is no supervisory authority.

Traditionally, items like American depository receipts (ADRs) (which represent stock that is traded on a foreign exchange), bank bonds, or derivatives have been traded on OTC markets. The entities trading in these markets have often been smaller companies that don’t meet the requirements to be listed in an official exchange, or that want to avoid associated fees. There is a risk in not having a supervisory exchange, but also more freedom and potential for reward - which is perhaps why larger companies, like Chinese multi-media giant Tencent or Nestle, the largest food company in the world, also trade on OTC markets (Murphy). 

### What does OTC look like for cryptocurrency trading?

There are quite a few cryptocurrency OTC desks, including Cumberland, Jump, and Circle. Moreover, bigger cryptocurrency exchanges are increasingly offering their own OTC platforms. Many of these exchange-related OTC desks have a very high entrance thresh hold, though. For example, a recently published Coin Telegraph article by Elijah Bradley notes that the Binance OTC desk will only facilitate transactions higher than 20 BTC ($69,552), and the Bittrex desk only supports trade commitments of $250,000 or more. 

This push to offer OTC market platforms is probably due to the fact that OTC is wildly popular among the crypto community. In April 2018, the OTC market handled between $250 million and $30 billion in trades every day, while exchanges managed just $15 billion, according to a report from the Digital Assets Research and TABB Group (Bradley). 

### So who uses Over-the-Counter markets in the cryptocurrency space?

While OTC markets traditionally were most often used by smaller companies with barriers to formal exchanges, in the realm of digital currency the key users are often “whales” - those with huge coin holdings.

This is because OTC transactions can be used for entities with large holdings to trade privately so that their movements do not cause huge markets shifts with public reactions to the knowledge of the trade. In the realm of cryptocurrencies, this often looks like one person trading via OTC desks, brokers, or even Skype and Telegram in order to make deals on their own terms (ie, not regulated by an exchange) and without risking price fluctuations to the entire currency (Bradley).
 
In the cryptocurrency market, there is an awareness that watching for the movement of whales is a smart move to figure out how to guard your own assets. Especially given the relatively new nature of the market and lack of diversity in well-established currencies, investing services tend to emphasize that the movements of individuals are fairly reliable weather maps (Dale). 

While transactions are meant to be private, there are still ways to monitor whales trading OTC. You can keep an eye on market caps, and look at data for top wallets in a network. Generally, if you see shifts that take longer and don’t follow public successes or failures of the network, then it could be the result of an OTC trade (Dale).

However, whales are not the only demographic trading in OTC markets. According to Bradley’s article, there have been suggestions that many Over-the-Counter traders are from Asia, with Cumberland citing in October 2018 that most of its OTC desk transactions occurred during the business hours of Asian timezones. Institutional investors and hedge funds are also becoming increasingly involved in crypto OTC markets. Some believe this is another reason why, recently, there has been such a push for formal exchanges creating OTC desks: because there is an expert belief that institutional clients will drive mass adoption of cryptocurrencies (Bradley). 

### Outlooks + Applications 

Alongside the influx of more traditional clients and exchange-based OTC desks are burgeoning technologies meant to increase ease-of-use and privacy, the other drivers behind OTC adoption. Paradigm, for instance, is an automated system for OTC traders that sets a standard workflow for price negotiation and deal settlement. 

The increased interest in Over-the-Counter markets has been followed by explorations of DEX solutions to the problems of formal, centralized exchanges. Groups like Gnosis, for example, are developing decentralized exchanges that use ZK-snarks to enhance privacy for trading. Is there a future where DEX offerings overtake the current popularity of OTC markets? 

OTC allows for deals that trade huge amounts of money to be made with a simple Internet message and potentially total privacy. To many, this may seem like the freedom we desire and are owed in our financial transactions. However, as regulatory bodies in countries like the US increasingly scrutinize the movement of cryptocurrencies, it is possible that new technologies enhancing anonymity and individual power in trading will see backlashes from traditional centers of power. But is there truly legal ground for this? Should there be?
