### Cosmos Stake Distribution and Validator Centralization

On September 5, 2019, Felix Lutsch of Chorus One took a snapshot of Cosmos Hub token holder and validator data in order to analyze the stake distribution in one of the first live Proof-of-Stake networks. Lutsch found some curious results, the most prominent of which was a lack of validator diversification on the network. 

Lutsch used the Stargazer API to analyze active delegations, as well as the corresponding size and validator addresses. An important caveat is that addresses are not necessarily one entity - the analysis was not able to account for whether or not one entity held multiple holder addresses.

At the time data was collected, the researcher saw “11,387 active delegations from 6,459 addresses on the Cosmos Hub.” Lutsch cites the average address as having 1.76 active delegations, which means very little diversification among delegators to different validators. 

Lutsch also noted that the top three most delegated-to validators s charge little-to-no  commission fee. The top validators by number of delegations and by amount of stake either fall into the less than 5% commission fee range, or greater than 10%. There are very few mid-range commission fee validators in the top 15% of validators. 

Validators that charge a very low fee are extremely popular among smaller holders, which makes a lot of sense. There are some curious outliers, though. For instance, Cosmostation, which is ranked at 7 in terms of amount of stake, has a validator fee of 12%, but also has one of the highest amounts of delegators, at 678. Lutsch surmised that it could be their high-quality wallet that brings so many delegators with relatively small holdings to the validator, even with the high fee. 

Diversifying the validators an address delegates to seems more popular for addresses that represent large stake holders. At the time of data collection, only 26.06% of addresses had staked with more than one validator. However, these addresses held around 5 times as many coins on average. 

More equal stake distribution and validator diversification are important for fighting centralization. The report cites the Cosmos Gini co-efficient as 0.7366 when the data was pulled. This Gini coefficient speaks to the distribution of stake across validators, with closer to 1 meaning more centralized, and closer to 0 meaning more equally spread. 

That’s not a wild number - according to a recent analysis, Ethereum’s Gini co-efficient is 0.798, and Bitcoin Cash is 0.754 (Parsiq). But it’s still not ideal, especially given the standard set by [the recent analysis of Edgeware’s token distribution](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/102), which gave a Gini-coefficient of 0.694. This is perhaps one of the factors that led to community members suggesting a validator diversification award proposal based on Gini-coefficients. 

### A Proposal for Validator Diversification Rewards

The basic premise of [the proposal](https://forum.cosmos.network/t/cosmos-validator-diversification-reward-draft-proposal/2701), submitted by Jesse Livermore to the Cosmos forum, is to edit the staking rewards algorithm to calculate a Gini co-efficient for each address in order to assess the diversity of its stake delegation. There would be a threshold Gini co-efficient given that allows the address to receive an additional staking reward for diversification. 

Livermore states that since each validator thus far has had comparatively good uptime and a minimal penalty for lack of liveness, there is little motivation to diversify among validators. However, the author stresses that low diversification now will lead to high centralization in the future, which is a clear security issue.

Livermore lays out several challenges in the forum post. First, artificial diversification would be a very real issue. If a validator has multiple addresses but does not publicize their ownership, there is no way to know that multiple validator addresses are run and managed by the same person or entity. A commenter on the post expands this argument to state that validator cartels could arise, where validators group up and offer an additional incentive to people willing to diversify among their group. The group would be the minimum amount of validators needed for a certain amount of stake to fit within the Gini co-efficient threshold and thus receive the block reward. This sort of cartel forming could actually lead to more centralization - although the original author strongly argues against this point, pointing out that the commenter is actually one of the top validators on the Cosmos network, and therefore motivated against this proposal (mattharrop). 

Another issue is that the reward has to be large enough to actually entice people to diversify the addresses they delegate to. This challenge speaks to an issue elucidated in the original Chorus One piece. Lutsch muses that UX structure, increased research needs, or other ease-of-use problems might be causing participants not to delegate across diverse addresses. 

The original author of the proposal also brings up the issue that large validators would be incentivized to oppose this sort of proposal, as it would probably decrease their commission amount as it is more probable that stake would be delegated to smaller validators. Another argument against the proposal is the potential for it to increase Atom inflation rate, though commenters suggested that the extra reward inflation could be countered with a reduction in staking award for those who do not fit under the Gini coefficient threshold (Ddrdrck).

The same top validator who explained their qualms regarding cartel-formation also made the case that the proposal would easily benefit many of the largest token holding delegators, as they can most easily diversify (mattharrop). According to the validator, this could potentially lead to a worse stake distribution across all accounts. 

Many also questioned the use of a Gini coefficient itself. One commenter suggested that a 50:50 split of delegated stake across 2 validators, which would be seen as perfectly equal with a Gini coefficient of 0 in this measurement, should not necessarily be seen as superior to a delegation across 4 validators at the ratios of 40:15:15:15:15 (which, using this measurement, would indeed have a higher Gini co-efficient) (FelixLts). Other community members voiced similar concerns. 

There are alternative inequality indexes to a Gini coefficient, Generalized Entropy Indexes, Atkinson Index, Piesch index, and the Kakwani index (Scorzafave). However, each index measures different things. For instance, the Generalized Entropy Index splits populations into subgroups and then adds measures of weighted inequality between groups to the inequality between groups. This measure is highly sensitive to changes in either extreme end of the distribution spectrum (Stata). Meanwhile, Atkinson’s Index gauges the total amount of income necessary for a community to give up in order for each member to have an equal share. It is well acknowledged that each measure of inequality has trade-offs, with Gini being relied on mostly because it is easy to assess in a standard way (DSPAU). 

### Application 

It is clear that even integrating a simple, Gini co-efficient-based diversification reward into a protocol brings up a lot of challenges. However, a reward for diversifying delegations is an important idea, given that lack of diversity now could certainly lead to centralization and security problems later.  For a single protocol, integrating a way to measure inequality into a staking reward could warrant experimentation with which measure of inequality would best serve to decentralize stake and diversify validators. 

Should all PoS protocols integrate diversification incentives? What could that experimentation look like? A curious approach to this could be implementing multiple types of incentive mechanisms for different groups on one network, then seeing which groups achieve better compliance and security.  But could there be better ways to tackle this problem than with changes to staking rewards? 

And on the flipside, is validator centralization really an issue at all? Are there ways to ensure network security and validator effectiveness while allowing some validator centralization?
