A big driver behind all blockchain development is the dual promise of transparency and privacy. A blockchain, after all, is a ledger meant to permanently describe all interactions that occur on the chain network. What sort of transparency that description should offer is open to experimentation, but there is widespread agreement that blockchain transactions should be as anonymous as possible. 

### What is the best way to do privacy?

General practices for anonymity include using pseudonymous identities, finding ways to create a verifiable and un-forge-able signature, and securely storing transaction data. There are a few ways to implement these privacy strategies. Mixing services are a prominent example. Traditional mixers have been implemented to tumble coins from transactions together, making it impossible to trace who the original payer of a coin was (Clifford). Meanwhile, a mixnet like that proposed first by David Chaum and implemented recently by NYM technologies routes transaction data through several nodes, which each mix and bounce multiple transaction datasets (Web3 Foundation). This makes it nearly impossible to determine where transactions were coming and going to. 

A variation on the mixing idea called ring signatures applies the scrambling idea to digital signatures. The mechanism groups together signers and only reveals that the signature comes from the group, not which member of the group it was made by. Another way of obfuscating identity is the generation of one-time-use, randomly produced “stealth addresses” (Clifford).

Homomorphic encryption, on the other hand, uses an algebraic system to allow data to be processed and operated on without revealing any information about the data (Crane). Zero-Knowledge Succinct ARguments of Knowledge, or zk-SNARKs, can be used for a similar purpose: proving a fact about something, without actually revealing the information that proves that fact. Other exciting privacy innovations include Secure Multi-Party Computation and Attribute-Based Encryption (Zhang). 

The ways to create privacy are constantly developing with the evolution of the blockchain ecosystem. With the widespread use of cryptocurrency has come notable privacy coins like Zcash, named one of ten breakthrough technologies of 2018 by MIT, as well as Monero, which uses ring signatures and has seen a lot of hype around its RandomX PoW algorithm meant to minimize the necessity of specialized hardware for mining (tevador). With the advent of DAOs, new challenges have come up, leading to the creation of [a language for privacy called Semaphore and other anonymity mechanisms](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/96). Meanwhile, increasing uses for smart contracts are prompting more and more groups to integrate total confidentiality directly into the structure of smart contracts with Trusted Execution Environments, [like pLibra](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/108).

### Where does the argument for privacy come from?

When considering all of these privacy mechanisms, an obvious question to ask is simply, why do we need privacy?

Perhaps the easiest argument to make is the necessity of privacy for cryptocurrencies. The cryptocurrencies generated by blockchain networks need to be completely anonymous so that a coin cannot have a known history and therefore carry the weight of its history. Otherwise, coins that have been involved in illicit activities, for example, would be looked at as less valuable because the owner takes on the responsibility for those coins theoretically being stolen or involved in an investigation - a phenomenon described as “tainted coins.” Thus, the anonymity or privacy of a coin itself is necessary for fungibility, an essential feature of sound money (Clifford). 

Beyond applications to cryptocurrencies, however, the culture of demanding true anonymity in the blockchain ecosystem goes all the way back to the 1970s. Before then, encryption was limited to mostly military use. In 1976, however, the publication of “New Directions in Cryptography” by Dr. Whitfield Diffie and Dr. Martin Hellman described an impending revolution in cryptography as new security measures would be needed to manage the boom of accessible technology globally (Horizen Academy). Throughout the 80s, Dr. David Chaum published several papers that advanced this revolution.

In his 1983 paper “Blind Signatures for Untraceable Payments,” Chaum began by explaining that there are dangers to totally private payment systems, including “lack of proof of payment, theft of payments media, and black payments for bribes, tax evasion, and black markets.” Despite this problem, Chaum laid out mechanisms to build a payment system based on a few foundational principles: the system must preclude third parties from ascertaining data about the payee, time or amount of payments made by a person, it must have the capacity to provide proof of payment or proof of payee identity for extreme cases, and it must have the ability to stop any payments determined to use stolen funds (Chaum). 

This framework forms the basis of cryptocurrency security today, though we still haven’t figured out the best ways to put the principles into practice perfectly in many cases. The implementation of these privacy measures differs across networks, and new technology like smart contracts, DAOs, and dApps make implementation questions more complex. Consider, for example, the very real concern about [the potential for Dark DAOs](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/98), which would use un-traceable transactions and the immutability of the blockchain ledger to form vote-buying cartels. 

Vote-buying and other forms of bribery are separate issues from privacy, however. The main reaction to Chaum’s work on foundational cryptography applications was not hesitance, but excitement. This excitement eventually led to the development of the Cypherpunks movement of the 1990s. One of the founders of the cultural shift, Eric Hughes, elucidated the goals of the movement in his paper “A Cypherpunk's Manifesto”:

“Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age. Privacy is not secrecy. A private matter is something one doesn’t want the whole world to know, but a secret matter is something one doesn’t want anybody to know. Privacy is the power to selectively reveal oneself to the world...An anonymous system empowers individuals to reveal their identity when desired and only when desired; this is the essence of privacy.” 

This is the spirit that defines the movement towards privacy in the blockchain ecosystem today. However, as suggested above, applying these foundational principles to the advanced technology we have now can get a bit tricky. 

For instance, an important part of the Cypherpunk Manifesto is the idea that a transaction should only necessitate sharing the minimum amount of data absolutely needed to complete said transaction. But a platform like Facebook that uses personal data to create a more personalized interaction experience begs the question, how do you define the minimum amount of data *needed* for a platform that doesn’t offer a necessary service? 

Of course, with a site like Facebook in particular, you can easily argue that those who participate are buying into a system where they should at the very least be allowed to tell the platform how much of their data they want to share. But if you’re trying to create standards for how much data is required for a type of service transaction, there is no clear answer for how to define the requirement. 

Despite the difficulty in deciding how to apply privacy mechanisms appropriately, there is a steadfast call for implementing privacy technologies that echoes the Cypherpunk Manifesto of the 1990s. As we keep developing technologies, it is increasingly important to not get too caught up in the excitement that has been percolating and driving innovation since Chaum’s original work. Instead, it is important to focus on the concrete real-world use cases, motivations, and technological innovations that drive the desire for privacy.
