Earlier this month, Laura Shin published a podcast titled “Althea Network: How Ethereum Is Used in the Real World.” The episode covers how the Althea network is serving as a decentralized Internet provider, allowing neighbors to support community Internet access using pay-as-you-go stablecoin plans (Shin). While Althea is an innovative alternative to traditional ISPs, the title of the podcast episode reveals an interesting disconnect in the blockchain ecosystem: the idea that blockchain technology exists separately from the “real world.”

### The Adoption Problem

This attitude largely stems from the lack of adoption of blockchain technology by what is sometimes labeled the “traditional” world. This points to a problem that many in the blockchain space have identified and are fighting against: the high barrier to the ecosystem given the level of technical knowledge needed to build and use blockchain-based technologies. As Aidan Hyman elucidated at Web3 Summit 2019, "If we want real people building real stuff, we need to abstract this technology to a place where not just subject matter experts are building, but people are building this for the world, for themselves" (Web3 Foundation). 

This problem is not new. In fact, David Chaum faced the same problem when he first introduced cryptocurrency in the form of eCash in the 1990s. “It was hard to get enough merchants to accept it, so that you could get enough consumers to use it, or vice versa,” Chaum told Forbes in 1999, after DigiCash had finally filed for bankruptcy. “As the Web grew, the average level of sophistication of users dropped. It was hard to explain the importance of privacy to them” (Wirdum). 

### Solutions: Regulation, Standards, and Infrastructure-level Integration

There are two main ideas circulating about how to solve the adoption problem. The first is that standard blockchain regulation is needed to build public trust for the technology. As Julian-Andre Winter, head of asset management at The Naga Group, has stated, “regulation is the first key for establishing blockchain technology at it will provide the lacking level of trust” (Fitzpatrick). Legal regulation via the state could be one way to imbue trust in the tech, leading to widespread use. However, current regulation efforts are lukewarm. According to Forbes, in the US only 17 states are working to pass regulatory laws, and those don’t address smart contracts at all (Fitzpatrick). There is also pushback in the blockchain community that compliance with current power structures defeats the purpose of building decentralized tech entirely.

Christian Ellul, director at E&S Group, supports a different kind of standards: internal industry best practices. In his words, the blockchain space “needs common standards to make the digital space more easily accessible.”  Ellul argues that setting these sorts of standards will lead to educational opportunities and frameworks that will build these standards and thus bring together world markets (Fitzpatrick). 

Perhaps more crucial to integrating blockchain technology into the real world is the need to truly integrate it as an infrastructure-building mechanism, not a product. As Michael Zargham explained in his Web3 Summit address, infrastructure is meant to be something we don't see and that unlocks human potential. As Allen Lee, the founder of QLC Chain, believes, “the day blockchain technology is used in day-to-day life is the day when people stopped talking about blockchain” (Fitzpatrick). 

### Applications 

There are some predictions of how this shift might happen in the future. In their paper from June this year “Blockchain adoption: A value driver perspective,” researchers Jannis Angelis and Elias Ribeiro da Silva assess the evolution of blockchain technology. We’ve already seen blockchain-based platforms focused on transactions, and we’re now in the space where smart contracts and then decentralized applications are more highly used. Angelis and Ribeiro da Silva believe that the next step will be AI-driven decision making for decentralized processes.

This expected step has enormous potential. Applying artificial intelligence to histories of how decision-making processes have resulted in order to build smarter could unlock huge amounts of human potential. However, harking back to Zargham and Lee’s points, this technology will have to be integrated into our world in a way that lets people take advantage of the results, without struggling to use the technology.  

It is promising that blockchain technology is widely moving away from just cryptocurrency applications, and into broader mechanisms that can be integrated into current technologies and infrastructural frameworks in society. Regulation probably is necessary for widespread adoption and true movement into the “real world.” However, given the clear disjunction between the principles of centralized regulatory authorities and the community values of decentralization, privacy, and freedom in the blockchain space, this will not be a smooth transition.
