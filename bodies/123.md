### What’s in identity on the blockchain?

Exploration of what identity means in the blockchain space is founded on two definitions: that of decentralized identity, and that of self-sovereignty. They are closely related terms, with a distinct lack of standardized definitions. 

Decentralized identity (DID) typically refers to an identity that is not managed or otherwise controlled by any central authority, giving the ID subject complete ownership of the identity. The working group on DIDs, Decentralized Identity Foundation, defines DIDs specifically as “Decentralized identities are anchored by blockchain IDs linked to zero-trust datastores that are universally discoverable” (Kames). 

Self-sovereign identity, on the other hand, refers to the power for the owner of the ID to have the ability to store their identity data on their own devices and prove their identity at their own volition. If someone wants to validate your self-sovereign identity, you have the power to prove that it’s you, without relying on a third-party centralized database. 

### Why do we need decentralized identities and self-sovereignty?

An article written by Vinny Lingham, the co-founder and CEO of Civic, describes how most institutions - governments, schools, banks, stores - have inadvertently become responsible for the management of identities linked to a vast amount of personal information (Lingham). Blockchain-based DIDs are a solution in that they put control over identity more readily into the hands of individuals, with no necessary need to share and limited possibility for remote hacking. 

Hacks are a big motivation driving that need to give people control over their identities. Lingham cites two recent hacks that prove this demand: the 2017 Equifax data breach that revealed valuable personal information from over 147 million identities, and the 2018 Cambridge Analytica breach, in which led to the misuse of poorly protected data from an estimated 87 million user profiles (Lingham). 

Beyond currently illegal breaches of personal data, there are also a lot of qualms about how data is used in the legal realm. Without true consent or permission, personal data is constantly harvested for use in ads and other psychology-based, profit-seeking campaigns that truly affect real-world behavior, often on a subconscious level. At Web3 Summit 2019, Edward Snowden explained why he thinks this leveraging of data is one of the key problems faced by human societies, stating:

“I think one of the biggest vulnerabilities in our system, the vulnerability that is being exploited by all these forces [leveraging data] is identity. In the modern world we are not permitted a sense of ownership without identity." 

In Snowden and many others’ views, a centralized identity should not be necessary for the many everyday interactions we currently need it for. Instead, identity should be self-sovereign, offering a way for an individual to selectively prove their person-hood and rights without having to reveal personal information. 

### How can we use DIDs to get control of our personal data?

Public/private key pairs found the basis of current DID practices. Public keys can be distributed to anyone, but only the individual has access to their own private key. This allows public keys to encrypt information, which the pair private key is only able to decrypt, and for authentication, which lets the public key verify the holder of the paired private key (Kames). 

There are a couple of commonly noted design challenges for decentralized identities. The first is the need not just for people being able to create their own identities, but also for integration into real-world areas - as in, your DID needs to be accepted at hospitals, by the police, and even at the local bar. This speaks to an adoption challenge for all blockchain-based technology: the current technological barriers. This is hard to solve because it requires collaboration and consensus on how to treat DIDs, which requires asking those organizations that need to verify your identity to integrate methods of accepting DIDs (Dragonchain). 

This brings us to the second design challenge: the necessity of having a standard, generic fix for how DIDs will be accepted. Part of this challenge includes the necessity of being able to access DIDs on different accounts across devices. Microsoft is introducing an open-source project called Ion, built on the Bitcoin blockchain, to accomplish this goal and enable DID integration on all of its products (Cuen). 

But despite Microsoft’s example, even if a standard is set, there is no clear way for organizations to be required to accept DIDs. What would an individual do if the provider of the service they wanted to purchase didn’t accept their DID? There isn’t yet a clear way for individuals to avoid needing to forego the service, or to create a second account and identity with that organization (Dragonchain). 

### Another roadblock to decentralized identity: KYC

Know Your Customer (KYC) is a mechanism that centralized institutions sometimes implement that require individuals to submit additional proof of identity, like a selfie or a passport photo. Banks and other regulated institutions are required to implement KYC rules - which means that DIDs that don’t allow for some form of KYC could face huge adoption issues. 

There have been arguments that blockchain-based KYC is a way to put identity in the hands of the individual, allowing them to input their additional proof-of-identity to a blockchain that the bank can access to verify it’s them, without seeing their data. However, many are against this idea, not least because it infringes on the decentralized nature of the identity if the user is required to get approval from a central chain-based authority. Even more importantly, KYC diminishes the ability for a self-sovereign design mechanism. 
### Applications

One option currently being explored in the industry is the idea of generating ID tokens. Dragonchain is creating a way for users to generate ID tokens each time they use their identity to log in to an account on a specific device, with the ability to manage their tokens through on the chain. 

Other groups are implementing similar solutions. Least Authority, for instance, is introducing the Zero Knowlege (ZK) Access Pass, which is based on Privacy Pass, a ZK cryptocurrency protocol. The ZK Access Pass gives anonymous tokens after a single proof of identity that can then be used for service elsewhere (Web3 Foundation). An interesting proposal has been made to extend this idea to homeless populations, which are inherently decentralized. A person could have a social worker verify their DID once, for instance, then be issued specific “service tokens” linked to their DID for future use (Varley & Wong). 

In a case that has risen due to politics rather than technical innovation, Catalonia has started building IdentiCAT, a blockchain-based, self-sovereign identity system. Details for the system are sparse, with most media coverage focusing on the social statement of Catalonia stepping away from Spanish central authorities, rather than this exciting jump of a traditional government into innovative blockchain-based technology (Lanz). 

The need for self-sovereign identity is important, but there are clear challenges in designing identities that are both decentralized and self-sovereign, that will *actually* be adopted to the real world. As this development process slowly continues, it will be interesting to see if the principles behind creating decentralized and self-sovereign identities can be maintained.
