### Takeaways

- Crypto wallets have developed beyond a bare-bones tool for sending and receiving money, to mechanisms that optimize the experience of using the blockchain-based currency, organizations, and applications that make up the Web 3.0 ecosystem
- As DAOs and dApps evolve, wallets have been and will continue evolving with them. There are currently some challenges to adopting these Web 3.0 wallets, however, including troublesome login processes and trade-offs between security and ease-of-use 
- Smart contract wallets that are vulnerable to bugs but secure their funds with a protocol-based insurance platform like Nexus Mutual are a prime example of some of the current challenges and innovation in the Web 3.0 wallet game

### Wallets in the Blockchain Ecosystem 

Wallets are crucial for interacting with blockchains - and right now, as blockchain-based technologies are rapidly evolving, so is the wallet ecosystem. Check out my closer look at the recent article, “An Overview of the Crypto Wallet Landscape,” from 1confirmation’s Richard Chen.  

### A Brief History of Crypto Wallets 

Chen begins his account by describing some of the first digital wallets. These were initially created in a way that was very transparent about the cryptography beneath the surface: the existence of private and public keys. Early on, the method was to write down your mnemonic, or seed phase, that locked the wallet - but that’s not very secure, and it’s not reasonable to ask people to memorize a random grouping of words and numbers. Projects like BrainWallet evolved to let people choose their own seed phrases, but perhaps as expected, people are too predictable and hackers early on were able to steal millions of dollars worth of Bitcoins (Chen). 

Wallets have since improved their user experience so that you don’t need to use your private key every time you want to make a transaction. Instead, you exchange the key with the wallet, and the wallet presents you with a simple user interface that allows you to send and receive money. 

The first generation of crypto wallets required users to download the wallet to their desktops, where it would either connect to a node or run a light client. Both of these options took time, and so the next generation of wallets focused on supporting mobile and web-based interfaces. These are software wallets, and today, there are many types with many variations, ranging from supporting different kinds of cryptocurrencies to offering mixing services to allowing for the purchase (not just exchange) of cryptocurrencies. While many wallets specialize in one of these niches, notable exceptions are Chinese wallets like im.Token, which seeks to combine all features into one app (Chen). 

In addition to software wallets, today there are many popular hardware wallets. These wallets are offline, often kept in safety deposit boxes or other secure locations, and require physical access for transactions. Hardware wallets can integrate innovations like the Ledger Nano X’s encrypted BlueTooth capabilities, or NGRAVE’s QR code communication. 

### What is a Web3 Wallet?

The original purpose of crypto wallets was pretty much the same as that of the wallets that we keep paper cash and coin in today: to let us store, give, and receive money. With the development of blockchain technology beyond cryptocurrency, however, the uses (and the importance of the uses) of crypto wallets is dramatically expanding.

As DAOs and dApps are being built on blockchain protocols, what was formerly a “crypto wallet” is now transforming into a device that facilitates unique participation in the decentralized Internet - Web 3.0. The wallet allows you to prove your identity and engage in interactions that are not always simply about sending and receiving wealth. Take the use case of Edgeware wallets, for instance. Your Edgeware wallet is not simply a place to store hard money - it allows you to participate in Edgeware governance, to create proposals from discussions, have a vote, and interact with your community. This wallet is not just about having cryptocurrency. This wallet is a crucial tool in the process of leveraging the blockchain ledger and crypto-economic incentives in order to optimize human organization. 

Chen’s article describes this transition primarily as a move from cryptocurrency wallets that support Web 2.0 applications to one that support Web 3.0 applications - which Chen describes as wallets that support web3.js, the Ethereum Javascript API. 

Chen’s point is important. As of 2018, approximately 95% of dApps were run on the Ethereum network, yet as of last month, even huge names in the crypto wallet space like BRD (which has received $54M in funding) didn’t support web3.js (Andrew, Chen). And wallets that did support web3.js,  like Metamask, have become increasingly popular (Chen cites that as of April 2019, Metamask had 264k monthly active users and was widely regarded as the dominant figure in the wallet space).

However, Chen’s description of Metamask as a “gatekeeper” to the Web3 dApp space and assertions that web3.js compatibility is a requirement for a wallet to be used in the Web3 space is a little bit misleading. This is simply because not all dApps are being built in Ethereum. In fact, increasingly more of the most popular dApps are built on other protocols. According to DappRadar, in the last week of October 2019, of the top ten dApps only three were run on Ethereum, with the others based on TRON, EOS, and ThunderCore. 

### Outlook on Web3 Wallet Integration 

Despite this key insight, it’s of course still true that web3.js is an important integration for wallets to give users access to the many dApps being built on Ethereum. Chen further offers some very helpful recommendations on how Web3 wallets could see more adoption and help with dApp adoption. 

Chen focuses heavily on possible improvements to Web3 Wallet UX, noting that the Web3 app experience should be ostensibly indistinguishable from the Web2 application experience. Specifically, Chen notes that according to discussions with developers, 90% of potential dApp users give up on the application when they are told they have to download the Metamask browser extension. Chen introduces a Web3 wallet SDK, which would allow dApps to be accessed with a simple username and password that links to a private key in the wallet, and enables password reset. In Chen’s mind, these are important features for meeting that goal of matching Web 2 applications. 

Chen also introduces the idea of a smart contract wallet and suggests implementing Social Key Recovery in order to ensure password access. However, Chen does note the possibility that a bug in the code would make it possible to hack the programmed smart contract wallet and steal funds. Argent and InstaDapp, two groups offering smart contract wallets currently, face this danger by employing Nexus Mutual, a protocol-based crowdfunded insurance platform. 

## Application 

There is another tradeoff Chen alludes to in his original Web3 wallet survey: whether to provide a wallet that is as secure as possible and allows for self-sovereignty, but is not very accessible to less technical users, or to offer a wallet that is designed for ease-of-use to those who are not familiar with the crypto space, but might not offer full privacy. It’s unclear to what degree current wallet-makers feel this trade-off, but it will certainly be important for future development to consider ways to avoid it and, instead, to align on industry standards for ensuring both ease-of-use and all of the independence, transparency, and security that blockchain tech can offer. This, of course, begs the question, to what degree is it possible to do both?

It is clear that Web3 wallets built for DAOs and dApps have a long way to go before they see widespread use and reliable best practices. However, with increased attention to the potential in decentralized organizations and applications, along with the push for broader smart contract employment across industries, wallet technology is in an exciting place with vast opportunities to optimize user interactions with Web3.0.
