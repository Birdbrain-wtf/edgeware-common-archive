## Takeaways

- Reputation is a powerful tool, and how to use it in blockchain technology is just beginning to be explored
- Some blockchain tech that leverages reputation includes Token Curated Registries, Reputation Curated Registries, Pseudonymous Reputation Bridges, and Reputation-Based Voting
- While there is good reason to be cautious, we should continue experimenting with how the powerful tool of reputation could be used to optimize human organization, governance, and development practices. 

### How to Use Reputation on Blockchains 

Reputation is powerful - and blockchain-based technology gives us unique new ways to explore how we might leverage that power to design better organizational structures. That’s a broad area, though, and one that has only seen early exploration efforts to far. So what are the intersection points of blockchain and reputation right now?

**Token Curated Registry (TCR)**

The idea for a Token Curated Registry is to use crypto-economic incentives to build a list that leverages a community’s collective knowledge in order to build a good list. Token Curated Registries were initially introduced by Mike Gouldin, working on adChain Registry, a TCR in which token holders verify that domain names are not fraudulent (Token Curated Registry). 

The list curated through a Token Curated Registry could be anything, blockchain-related or not - from best restaurants in Detroit to most reliable validators. TCRs could each have their own native currency and compete to attract the most and the best data, as well as the most skilled knowledge-builders. 

You could buy a token, put stake with your application to add something to the list. If someone challenges your application, it can be put to a vote. The deposit will be returned to the winning party (either the applicant or the person who initially challenged the application and those who voted against it split the stake). In original proposals for TCRs, voting would be token-weighted, meaning that each vote would be weighed by the amount of the native coin held (Token Curated Registry). 

**Reputation-Curated Registry**

A Reputation-Curated Registry is a curated registry where reputation weighs the votes on what to list, not stake. This type of registry was proposed originally by Stratis for a DAOstack DAO, which has a unique understanding of Reputation. In a DAOstack DAO, a participant’s “Reputation Score” determines their voting power. The ways to gain or lose reputation are different for each DAO, but common ones are voting with a passing proposal, or a community proposal to award an individual with Reputation awards (Weller). 

The originally imagined RCR, then, is a DAOstack DAO where the point of the organization is to use a reputation-based system to curate a specific registry. The author notes that you would need to embed a crypto-economic incentive for reputation-holders to participate in and honestly contribute to the list-building process, with suggestions for potential funding sources including an ICO, grant awards, or application fees (Stratis). 

For a very meta example, DAOstack actually has a DAO that is working to curate a list of Genesis-network approved DAOstack DAOs, called the arc-hive according to comments on the original RCR post (Stratis).

Stratis also notes that curating a registry based on reputation will not have the drawbacks that a TCR might, where more participants will not necessarily mean better curation, and could in fact just mean an influx of hopeful amateurs. 

**Pseudonymous Reputation Bridge** 

Balaji Srinivasan recently proposed a social media framework that would allow the use of followers to verify reputation. With this method, which Srinivasan calls a reputation bridge for pseudonymous identities, instead of applying and voting to create a certified and community-backed listing of some sort, a person can be given a signal of a good reputation simply by verifying their following in a community (StarkWare). 

This would be a simple way to attach a very basic indicator of reputation to identities, which could be used for a myriad of purposes from granting voting power to awarding preferred domain space. The Hive website, right now just a proof of concept, is currently using Twitter followers as one of their metrics for creating a list of top influencers in the crypto ecosystem - and is currently inviting developers to propose potential use cases for the reputation data they’ve assembled. 

**Reputation-Weighted Voting**

Beyond use for an RCR, the sort of reputation-based organization system suggested by DAOstack could be implemented broadly as a mechanism for reputation-weighted voting. However, to use reputation in this way first begs questions about the inherently abstract nature of reputation - a concern that applies to all of these potential implementations. Let’s say you were trying to build a reputation “profile” for someone. There are many parameters you could choose to use to analyze the person’s reputation, but even if you were able to settle on good parameters, the question of how to apply knowledge about those parameters to the reputation profile is difficult. 

A simple way to quantify reputation is to link an identity to a voting reputation that accounts for past voting actions and judges based on successes or failures. You could also try to quantify reputation by building a system that takes human inputs about a person’s reputation and analyzes them - although the judgment process probably would need a mix of human and automated decision-making to be successful.

This could possibly be implemented by using tokens as a means to weigh a set of reputation parameters. You could allow a space for people to submit testimony for their own or other’s reputations, and then allow a voting period to build a reputation profile. The voting mechanism could integrate crypto-economic primitives to encourage honest voting - like making token distributors put stake with their distribution proposal, or get rewards if other token distributors agree with their delegations. 

This system would run into many of [the challenges that smart contract oracles currently face](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/113), however - challenges that we don’t currently have defined best-practice solutions for. This system would also need to be constantly updated. Moreover, stories like those of Elizabeth Holmes, who used false information to build a deceptive reputation and consequently a billion-dollar blood-testing empire, should caution us to believe that reputation is an end-all, be-all tool. 

But at the same time, the abstract nature of reputation can allow it to help solve other abstract challenges. For instance, this sort of system would be invaluable for the issue Kevin Owocki brings up: [the problem of accurately gauging the work that developers do and appropriately remunerating them](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/116). No matter what, judging the value of a developer’s work is an abstract issue - but using a reputation-based system could help. 

## Application 

Tales like that of Elizabeth Holmes should give us pause as we begin to explore how knowledge about a person or entity can be leveraged. But they shouldn’t stop us from experimenting with how this powerful tool could be used to optimize human organization, governance, and development practices.
