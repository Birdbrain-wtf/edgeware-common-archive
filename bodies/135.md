## Takeaways 

- Stablecoins can be tied to fiat currency in a bank, cryptocurrency collateral, or supported by an algorithmic “central bank.” Each method has pros and cons, with trust problems ranging from dependence on an over-centralized issuer to reliance on an experimental algorithm.
- Stablecoins are often thought of as a type of cryptocurrency that is compliant or familiar enough to traditional financial norms to see mass adoption. However, many coins don’t actually embrace the dual promise of transparency and privacy that blockchain offers.
- Recent criticism of Tether’s lack of transparency and the possibility that their dishonest minting strategy led to serious market manipulation and the 2017 Bitcoin boom demonstrates some of the major problems with stablecoins. 

### What are stablecoins and why should we care?

Stablecoins are cryptocurrencies that are tied (or pegged, or tethered) to another kind of asset. The coins are named “stable” because they are designed to sit right in that sweet spot between crypto market volatility and fiat money unreliability. 

The stablecoin market is big - last February, the market value was at $3 billion, twice the size it was a year previously. They’ve gotten a lot of attention recently in the global sphere because Libra is meant to be a stablecoin, tethered to a big basket of other assets. But questions about transparency and trust for stablecoin issuers have led to questions about whether stablecoins do any good for the wider crypto ecosystem - or if they should even be considered part of the ecosystem in the first place.

### How do stablecoins work?

There are three popular stablecoin models for tethering a coin to another asset, including:

- Backing the stablecoin with fiat currency in a bank.
- Backing the stablecoin with cryptocurrency collateral.
- Backing the stablecoin with a “central bank” defined by algorithms (Orcutt).

Each of these methods has its own pros and cons. While backing the stablecoin with cash in the bank is perhaps a more familiar way to collateralize the coin to the wider public, it also requires centralization. This means always trusting a third party, the stablecoin issuer, to provide accurate information about that tether and to issue coins honestly.

Backing the stablecoin with a cryptocurrency takes away some of that trust by conducting the interaction over a blockchain, but it also situates the coin fully within crypto markets, meaning it might not avoid the volatility it was meant to. Groups like MakerDAO answer this problem with overcollateralization, but at some level, users are still just hedging their bets ([though the shift to Multi-Collateral Dai may change this dynamic](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/126)). 

Using an algorithm to manage the backing of a stablecoin is perhaps the most innovative method, but also the most risky. Projects like Basis are taking the lead on this approach, and there is certainly a lot of hope for it - the project raised $133 million in VC funds last year. However, the Basis example represents a system composed of two coins, the stablecoin and the “basis” coin, with the algorithm selling basis coins as bonds to users whose stablecoins drop below the $1 peg. After the stablecoin returns to its dollar peg, then bond basis tokens can be sold for the full dollar amount (Orcutt). Trusting an algorithm to do this work could ensure it runs incredibly smoothly, but it also requires that people trust the method and continue participating in the market, regardless of swings. 

### Critiques of stablecoins

As MakerDAO sets out, all stablecoins face the challenge of oracle truthfulness. The price of buying the coin will always depend on information from an oracle about the exchange rate between the stablecoin and the asset its pegged against, so having correct information is crucial (MakerDAO). But that can be a challenge, given that [oracles are not a perfectly designed technology](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/113).

According to the MIT Technology Review, Preston Byrne, a founder and former COO of the early blockchain startup Monax, is a very vocal critic of stablecoins for just this reason. To Byrne, it seems stablecoins ignore basic rules of economics. In his words, the fact that users who want to buy a stablecoin will always find it listed at a random price that keeps up the peg “ignores that in all market-based exchange, price is determined by a meeting of the minds of a buyer and seller, not by an algorithm...All that is required for these systems to fail is for people not to buy the product” (Orcutt).

Beyond the challenge of people failing to buy into the coins, there has also been recent controversy over how well stablecoins are (or aren’t) regulated. Tether, which is widely recognized as the most commonly used stablecoin, claims that each of its coins (USDT) are backed by fiat money in a bank. However, they have yet to publicly prove that fact - so nobody actually knows if the coin is truly pegged to the US dollar (Orcutt).

Scrutiny for Tether has only grown recently, as a new report suggests that the sharp peak in Bitcoin in 2017 was the direct result of a single whale propping up prices.  The study goes on to say that this was only possible because Tether was printing stablecoins not in response to more user demand (and more money in their reserve), but rather, was creating unbacked coins as a reaction to overall market downswings (MIT Technology Review).

## Application 

Stablecoins are often thought of as a type of cryptocurrency that is compliant or familiar enough to traditional financial norms to see mass adoption. However, many coins don’t actually embrace the dual promise of transparency and privacy that blockchain offers. Instead, they seem to either replicate the issues of centralization and trust that traditional currencies have, or simply serve as ways for users to hedge their bets on crypto markets. While that’s not necessarily a bad thing, it’s not that innovative, and at its worst it can allow users to attempt market manipulation. 

Algorithmic stablecoins could be the future for this market, but they also require a good amount of trust in the process. In the context of the transparency problems with the biggest stablecoin on the market, however, perhaps people are ready to trust an algorithm to deliver a genuinely stable currency.
