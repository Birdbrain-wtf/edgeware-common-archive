### What is a Curation Market?

Curation Markets, originally introduced by Simon de la Rouviere, are meant to allow markets to coordinate on a goal or topic with value. This goal or topic can be anything from a meme (by its original definition, a unit of cultural value), to a trait you want to see in your community. Each curation market is formed around one of these specific ideas, and a token associated with the market can be used to meaningfully evaluate the value and the curation of the goal or topic (Token Engineering).

It all sounds a bit abstract, so I’ll give an example that de la Rouviere recently explained on an Epicenter podcast. Let’s say Cape Town, South Africa, was experiencing a drought (as it has recently), and conserving water became a desirable trait, or a goal for the town. You could create a curation market around saving water. Restaurants that save water could buy “water conservation” tokens, which would let them receive more business as more people around town see that they have this desirable trait. Individuals would want “water conservation” tokens, too, as there would be a healthy amount of public shame associated with not conserving water (Epicenter). 

Nobody would be able to fake having “water conservation” tokens, as this would all be recorded on a blockchain. The token would also have a continuous pricing model according to a bonding curve - which means, basically, that as more people buy tokens and the number of tokens increases, the price to get a token also increases. This makes it advantageous to buy into the water conservation game early on, when it doesn’t cost as much to prove that you’re being a good citizen. 

A key part of the bonding curve model is that you don’t just buy the tokens at a certain price - you can sell them, too. So in our example, when somebody in Cape Town buys a water conservation token, they aren’t purchasing it from anybody in particular. Instead, they lock their money in a smart contract as a sort of deposit to enter the market. When they decide to leave the market, however, they don’t just get all the money back. Instead, there is also a “sell curve,” which determines the price at which you can exchange your curation market tokens for. 

This example does reveal a potential flaw in the most basic form of a curation market. Let’s say the poorest restaurant in Cape Town was the best at conserving water, but they don’t have the technological awareness to know about the water conservation curation market until the price of tokens is too high for them to buy in. The restaurant would be excluded from the market, and potentially lose business from it, despite their efforts to contribute to the water-saving effort. While on a small scale, using word-of-mouth to build reputation can work just fine to keep some of their customers, they might lose the business of anybody from outside of their small neighborhood, or any tourists, who can see on the curation market interface that the restaurant doesn’t have water conservation tokens. 

### What Are Some Example Curation Market Verticals?

Some notable examples of curation market implementations offered by Token Engineering include: 

- Relevant: Social news reader
- Ocean Protocol: Data & AI services substrate (uses "proofs" variant of CMs)
- oscoin: Compensating open-source creators
- Way Network: Social discovery
- Meme Factory: Game of curation & trading dank memes
- 1Hive: Project funding
- SingularityNet: Decentralized AI
- Zap Store: Decentralized data feed access & curation

Some of the more interesting, broader applications that de la Rouviere has brought up include creating a “Personal Attention” market, in which content producers buy participants tokens in order to prove that they are attention-worthy, and the potential for curation markets to be used for funding open-source development. De la Rouviere also suggests the potential for a decentralized band to be formed, or any sort of creative endeavor, with tokens used to decide how to fund different elements of the project, what sort of design choices to make, and any other communal decision-making. 

### Design Challenges for Curation Markets

The Cape Town issue of potentially disadvantaging participants who are less able to put stake in the market shows that a simple bonding curve-based curation market might not always be the best solution. This is perhaps one of the reasons that de la Rouviere suggests that, over time, different cost curves, beneficiary models, and other changes will probably be applied to the original curation market structure. With these changes, de la Rouviere offers that the most useful of these markets in the future could be a curation market for curation markets - a way to judge market models that attempt to achieve the same goal. A further step could be applying a prediction market model to this market of markets, or using AI to learn on the history of every curation market and guide their further development. 

The curation market is an interesting idea because it does rely on the fact that people joining the market are aligned on the goal. De la Rouviere allows that with the generic model, it is possible for people to enter the market who are not incentivized to curate honestly, but instead want to see how they might make money off of the model. With a huge inflow of people like this, the value of the market would be lost, which should be another consideration for changes to the generic set up (Epicenter). Good governance, therefore, is also a hugely important part of creating successful curation markets. 

## Application 

The goal of a curation market is to enable community collaboration and fast growth on concepts or goals that are of importance to a specific group. While there are clearly design challenges for these types of markets, they are foundationally an innovative variation on how to use reputation and blockchain technology to leverage the wisdom of a group and enhance communal decision-making.
