### What’s at Stake with the Istanbul Fork?

The Istanbul hard fork is the first of Ethereum’s planned upgrades on its journey to becoming Ethereum 2.0: a Proof-of-Stake platform with database sharding for increased scalability. The Istanbul hard fork, which was originally meant to be implemented in October but is now hopefully scheduled for December 4th, integrates six Ethereum Improvement Proposals (EIPs) (Paradigm). This is all just the beginning of the lead up to Ethereum 2.0, which is meant to be activated with the Serenity upgrade at some point in the next few years. 

Perhaps the most controversial element of Istanbul is the new mining algorithm, which will change Ethereum’s Proof of Work process to something called ProgPoW. According to a researcher from Nuo Network, the change to ProgPoW was approved by 100% of miners and 94% of token holders (Seth). However, it’s seen strong push-back in the community, because the change in mining algorithm will disadvantage ASIC miners. 

The Ethereum virtual machine, which facilitates the execution of smart contracts and subsequently the running of dApps, will be upgraded to Web Assembly Code and account versioning will be implemented to allow for breaking features to be applied while maintaining smooth execution of other smart contracts (eth.wiki). 

According to the Ethereum Wiki, the Istanbul fork was the last of its kind, in that EIP’s were developed for a planned forking schedule: a “fork-centric approach.” In the future, there is a plan in place to only schedule forks when EIPs have been fully developed, shifted emphasis to the proposals, rather than planned upgrade times (eth.wiki). 

There was some initial concern when the Istanbul hard fork occurred earlier than some expected, and notably, the Ropsten Ethereum testnet saw instability when a miner who was unaware of the fork continued developing the unforked version of the chain (Cant). Some concern also arose with the development that several hundred Aragon smart contracts would be broken by the upgrade, due to an increase in gas amount necessary for transactions that the contracts were not written to respond to (Kim). However, in both of these cases, it is widely agreed that the developers of the chains and contracts should be prepared for the changes accompanied by this sort of upgrade. It is the responsibility of everyone building on Ethereum to understand changes to the virtual machine.

### Does Ethereum Have a Viable Exit Mechanism?

Despite this popular sentiment that those building on Ethereum are necessarily and freely bound to its evolutionary process, there has long been concern percolating regarding the “unforkable” nature of Ethereum. 

This concern does not mean that Ethereum can’t be forked with planned upgrades - because it can. Rather, it implies that the nature of the network and DeFi makes it so that minority forks can’t be sustained. If the currency used by a minority fork isn’t recognized by popular exchanges (which realistically, it won’t be), then the smart contracts and dApps running on that minority fork become worthless - which essentially means that Ethereum has no viable exit mechanism, short of bowing out of the entire network (Lee & Qureshi). 

## Application

Perhaps this is a problem that will be solved by Ethereum 2.0 - or maybe not. The launch of Ethereum 2.0 is likely a long way off, and details about it are still very unclear. While the Serenity upgrade is meant to implement the first phase of Ethereum 2.0, Phase 0, it will both deploy an entirely new beacon chain and make changes to the Ethereum 1.0 chain. This is important: Ethereum 2.0 is not meant to supercede Ethereum 1.0, at least not for five to ten years (EthHub). Instead, the first two phases of the launch will have implications for both chains. After Phase 0 sees the launch a beacon chain, Phase 1 is meant to implement data sharding, while Phase 2 is meant to layer execution semantics on top of shards and truly finalize the existence of Ethereum 2.0 (Web3 Foundation).

It’s important to note that the development teams (there are nine working on the project) are moving very conservatively, and they themselves are still fuzzy on what the final product will look like. They’ve emphasized that they are hoping to get a lot of community input on what the capabilities and interface should look like (Web3 Foundation).
