## Takeaways

- The International Co-operative Alliance defines a co-operative as "**an autonomous association of persons united voluntarily to meet their common economic, social, and cultural needs and aspirations** through a jointly-owned and democratically-controlled enterprise." 
- Co-operative organizations have higher productivity levels, employee wages, and profits. **They incorporate all the ideals necessary for sustainable development, but they often lack the starting capital and appropriate governance models that can help them survive long-term.**
- **Co-ops could benefit hugely from the use of blockchain-based community organizing.** There is a culture match between the principles behind blockchain technology and the ideals of cooperativism, as well as a parallel between the opportunity for evolution necessitated by the co-operative model and [that provided by on-chain governance mechanisms](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/140-organizational-governance-mechanisms-for-blockchains). 

### What’s a co-op and where did they come from?

The International Co-operative Alliance (ICA) defines a co-operative as "an autonomous association of persons united voluntarily to meet their common economic, social, and cultural needs and aspirations through a jointly-owned and democratically-controlled enterprise." The idea of a co-op is ancient, but these organizations started to become formally structured and implemented throughout the 18th century, as increased urbanization meant people struggled to obtain basic food needs. When textile mill workers from Rochdale, England went on strike in 1843 and the strike failed, 28 people founded the Rochdale Equitable Pioneers Society to open a food store as an alternative to the company store. This was not the first official co-op, but it was the first to be successful long-term (Co+op). 

**Throughout the coming centuries, in periods of strong cultural dissatisfaction or economic failure, co-op movements subsequently took off as people learned the benefits of peer-to-peer marketplaces and organizing.** In 1848, co-ops begin to thrive in Europe, spurred on by disillusionment with the humanistic values of the French and US revolutions and protests across the continent (Bowman & Stone). Though many of these did not last, the co-op movement sprung up again in 1871 under the Paris Commune. In the 1930s, co-ops flourished across the US as the Great Depression hit communities hard. These co-ops were often electrical and housing co-ops (Co+op). 

In 1933 and 1936, FDR’s New Deal supported urban co-ops, with several successful co-ops launched that have survived until today. Through the late 1960s and 70s, another wave of co-ops opened across the US, often inspired by 60s counterculture and focused on principles of equality more than functionality (Co+op). More recent history has seen a similar combination of government encouragement and open opportunity spurring co-op development. 

For instance, in the early 1990s, following the withdrawal of Soviet credits from Cuba, semi-autonomous collective farm co-ops were supported by the government. At the same time, unemployed rural workers from Brazil began to occupy land and form their own agricultural co-ops (Bowman & Stone). Meanwhile, some co-ops have been more directly politically motivated: in 1994, Chiapas, Mexico, agricultural co-ops were started by the Zapatistas, who were seeking to fight globalization (Bowman & Stone).

**Co-operative organizations often have higher productivity levels, lower employee turnover rates, increased employee wages, and increased profits** (Anzilotti). They incorporate all the ideals necessary for sustainable development, but they often lack the developed governance structures that can help them survive long-term (that’s a hint - we’ll jump into how blockchain tech could help facilitate co-op development in a bit). 

### Why aren’t co-ops everywhere?

Communal ownership means that each individual in an organization has responsibility and stake in its operations and returns. **This has stark advantages over traditional business models, but it also brings unique challenges - namely, securing capital.** 

When people want to start a co-operative organization, or transition a traditional business to a co-operative one, they have to invest their own money. This means that if the business fails, they lose both their investment, and their job. It’s a big risk, and it’s one of the main reasons more businesses don’t start as co-operatives, despite the clear benefits. 

**However, some of the most successful corporations in the world are co-ops.** Take the Mondragon Coorperative Corporation in Spain. The Mondragon network includes industrial co-ops, a cooperative bank (the Caja Laboral Popular), a social security system, a student cooperative, agricultural co-ops, housing and construction co-ops, and consumer co-op stores. The Mondragon bank is often identified as a key part of the success of the network, as it provides capital and technical support to new co-ops. Another advantageous element of the Mondragon network is worker identification with Basque culture, which unites co-op members and is a point of solidarity across groups (Lindenfeld & Wynn).

### How could co-ops and blockchain technology intersect?

The emphasis on solidarity as a success factor should resonate with many in the blockchain space. A clear example of how this is concretely played out: a blockchain ecosystem culture that emphasizes the importance of privacy has made it possible for technology like Monero's ring signatures to be successful. Ring signatures require a group of people to cooperate in order to ensure the privacy of each group member when it comes time to use a digital signature. The ring signature reveals that the signature came from the group, not which member, and it only works because the group is willing to come together under the common cultural value of privacy. 

This is a really concrete parallel - but the broader cultural values of the co-op movement are also aligned with the goals of many projects in the blockchain space. **In particular, an emphasis in co-operative organizations on open membership, self-sovereignty, and independence speaks clearly to many of the principles that found the crypto world.** Further, as co-ops develop, there are clear calls for governance structures like those being explored in the blockchain space today. 

One of the key recommendations from the New Economics Foundation in their report on how to double the size of the UK’s co-op sector is to fund “the development of a core co-op API that could provide the building blocks of a common digital infrastructure.” **The infrastructure provided by on-chain governance models could make for the perfect digital platform to facilitate the creation or transition to a co-operative organization.** 

On-chain governance allows for constant re-assessment of values, financial models, and management structures. The definition of success for a co-operative organization is “the health of the cooperative network as such and the maintenance of job opportunities within the cooperative system for all members of the community” (Lindenfeld & Wynn). **This definition of success requires a governance model that is not hard-coded to maximize profit like that of a traditional business, but instead can change as the needs of the community change.** 

## Application 

Not only is there a culture match between the principles behind blockchain technology and the ideals of cooperativism, but there is also a parallel between the opportunity for evolution necessitated by the co-operative model and that provided by on-chain governance structures. **Moreover, the biggest struggle for co-operative organizations, raising capital, could potentially be solved with innovations that the blockchain space is leading in**: like the potential for [game-theorizing funding with Dominant Assurance Contracts](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/101), or the many ideas surrounding [how to fund open-source development](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/116).
