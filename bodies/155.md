## Takeaways

- In the US, for a DAO to be legally compliant it needs to be wrapped as a legally registered business. So far, this has looked like DAOs working with individual states to register as LLCs.
- OpenLaw is working to offer a library of other potential legal wrappers and automatic filing services to facilitate more straightforward onboarding to legal compliance 
- There are still many nuanced questions about what it means to be a legal DAO, especially regarding privacy laws and regulations in other parts of the world. 

### What are some legal DAOs?

In the US, for a DAO to be legally compliant it needs to be wrapped as a legally registered business. dOrg, which claims it was the first legally compliant DAO in the US, was formed based on Vermont’s Blockchain-Based Limited Liability Company (BBLLC) law. The BBLLC law makes it possible to “reference blockchain code (smart contracts in our case) as the legitimate source of authority for their company’s operations and governance” (Biggs). 

Meanwhile, Blockstack claims to have offered the first SEC-approved ICO in the history of the US, which opened on July 11th of this year. With the SEC’s Regulation A+ framework, the sale was open to both accredited and non-accredited investors. Blockstack Token, which facilitated the token sale and blockchain, is an LLC (Ali). 

OpenLaw followed a similar track, originally working with Aragon and New York State to set further precedence for a DAO wrapped in an LLC. They’ve recently turned that effort into a framework for “resurrecting The DAO,” in the form of a Limited Liability Autonomous Organization: The LAO. To comply with the SEC’s stance on ICOs, only accredited investors will be able to put stake in the OpenLaw LAO, as the coins spent to buy into the LAO will be viewed as security tokens (Byrne). OpenLaw is also offering a library of other potential legal wrappers and automatic filing services to facilitate more straightforward onboarding to legal compliance for DAOs (OpenLaw). 

The OpenLaw LAO is meant to mimic MolochDAO’s investment structure, but also be for-profit.  MolochDAO is not currently registered as LLC because the organization’s investments are not necessarily for individual profit, but rather go to projects the community deems to represent the common good. However, as MolochDAO introduced a second version of their DAO, they also suggested that members could spin up a MolochLAO to be for-profit (MolochVentures).

Tech attorney Preston Byrne has suggested that a DAO like the OpenLaw LAO should potentially be required to register as an investment business under the US Investment Company Act of 1940. Another question that has been raised is how accredited investors can be verified in a DAO. OpenLaw has suggested that an on-chain verification with an oracle service could be implemented, though Byrne has pushed back against the feasibility of this option (Byrne).

Others have shared concerns that a DAO LLC loses all of its decentralized and autonomous shine with regulatory limitation. The argument goes that the only real difference between the DAO LLC and a regular corporate venture is that the start-up capital can come instantly from the crypto-market, instead of from traditional credit and VC options (Kim). That said, the DAO LLC would still have the transparency, auditability, and accountability ensured by an org founded in blockchain tech - and instant funding is no small advantage, either. 

Beyond US financial regulatory bodies, DAO developers are also concerned with compliance to privacy laws in the rest of the world. Just last week, DAOStack leadership solicited legal advice on a blog post that questions who is in charge of privacy compliance of DAOs, how Data Protection by Design might be applied to DAOs, whether the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) applies, and the general state of DAO laws in the rest of the world beyond the US (Jantine). These questions show just how much is unexplored here. 

The question of DAO legality further extends beyond compliance to traditional regulation, to [how code can be seen as law](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/132-foundational-text-code-is-law) - both in that it is already regulating behavior in its design, and in that it has the potential to offer a better legal system than the one we already have. A clear example of a group toeing this line is Kleros, a DAO meant to offer decentralized dispute resolution services. The potential for DAO systems to replace traditional legal systems is currently echoing throughout the blockchain ecosystem, with particularly loud calls coming from Vlad Zamfir, who has written extensively about the topic, and Luis Cuende, who opened Web3 Summit 2019 by calling for a DAO-based legal system. 

## Application

As the potential applications of DAOs increases, it will be important to start exploring the more nuanced questions regarding their legality, especially in tackling [the adoption problem](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/122-why-isnt-blockchain-part-of-the-real-world).
