The implementation of a Harberger Tax in a DAO is a crazy experiment - one that could show us new ways to think about the value of money, assets, and human engagement. 

**A Harberger Tax is a standard tax percentage that applies to assets with self-assessed values.** It’s a concept that’s been made popular recently by Glen Weyl and Eric Posner, in their book *Radical Markets.* It’s also one of the founding principles behind RadicalxChange, a group started by Weyl and Vitalik Buterin. In that space, it is called the “Common Ownership Self-Assessed Tax (COST)” (Tang & Peng).

The idea is that the owner of an asset chooses its value, meaning they know how much will be collected in taxes on that asset. However, they have to balance the incentive to value their assets at a low price with the fact that, in the Harberger Tax system, anyone can buy an asset at any time if they pay the price the owner valued it at. The balance should allow assets to shift towards the people who genuinely value them and will use them in the most worthwhile ways, resulting in fewer monopolies across more democratic markets.  

**The system is designed in hopes of getting at the true value of an asset**, as defined by constructive use or otherwise meaningful engagement. NFT marketplaces like Decentraland, OpenSea, and Harberger Ads are already using the Harberger Tax system to run liquid markets for digital goods (Riady). If an Edgeware group wanted to spin up a DAO to facilitate any sort of Harberger marketplace, taxes could go to the DAO treasury, with the choice on how to use it left to the community.

The Harberger Tax system has also been suggested for DAO leadership. A council member could put a value on their leadership position, and anyone who can match that value will be able to take their spot (Heynes). In this case, the taxes could be collected daily for as long as the counselor was in a leadership position. The taxes could either be returned to the council member after they are deposed or could again go to the DAO treasury for community use. 

Tying stake to a leadership position, or an asset, presents its own challenges, though. Similar to [the challenges behind token bonding curves and Curation Markets](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/142-tests-and-opportunities-for-curation-markets), the Harberger Tax system could allow for wealthy individuals to maliciously take advantage of those who contribute meaningfully to the system. For instance, if an individual was genuinely more fit and passionate about the leadership role, or taking care of an asset, but didn’t have the initial funding to set its value very high, or couldn’t afford the taxes on it, then there’s nothing stopping a nefarious actor from buying the asset. Is there potentially a way to integrate an incentive structure or another governance mechanism to face this challenge?

## Application 

The idea of a tax system that can revolutionize the way we think about value and transact with each other is inspiring. However, a big barrier to adoption here is simply the current lack of widespread experimentation and deeper understandings of the implications of a new system. The potential to build Harberger DAOs gives us a unique opportunity to apply this novel tax system. Testing spaces and structures that a Harberger system works in will allow us to discover new meanings and uses for ownership, engagement, and value. 

Remaining questions:

- Are there other spaces besides in governance or a marketplace where the Harberger Tax System could intersect with a DAO? 
- Are there novel markets that a Harberger system could be applied to? 
- Are there other challenges to this sort of experiment? 
- Could this tax system be combined with other incentives for meaningful participation in a DAO?
