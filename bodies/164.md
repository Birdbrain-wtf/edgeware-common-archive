As we consider [the ultimate goals of token distribution](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/151-what-does-an-ideal-token-distribution-look-like), we might also think about what types of distribution mechanisms we can experiment with as we strive to reach those goals. To intentionally design some token distribution experiments, we might first look at wealth redistribution efforts by traditional governments, data on token use in Ethereum, diversely shaped token bonding curves, and the potential integrations of a community engagement reward mechanism. 

Some initial questions:

- Are there inevitable distribution states of major currencies/tokens? What might they be, and are they desirable?
- Does designing different origination structures result in stable or metastable long-term distribution results?
- If this isn’t a problem that can be managed from the bottom-up, can we manage it with a top-level mechanism?

## Outcomes of Original Distribution States

*Wealth Redistribution in Real-World Governments*

For our first example to ask whether or not major currencies have an inevitable distribution state, let’s take a look at one of the most graspable examples we have: modern country economies. Researchers from MIT have shown that economies that start with a more equal income distribution will see less wealth redistribution efforts. Wealth redistribution efforts can be seen as a way to combat centralization, a reaction to too much money pooling in too few hands. Because its initial wage distribution is more equally distributed compared to most capitalist economies, Japan’s government does not do much work to change wealth distribution. Socialist planned economies are largely the same: the government does not need to engage in wealth redistribution efforts because the people *don’t* have any capital and land income, and *do* have set wage rates (Rossner & Barkley). 

So, here’s some broad-scale evidence that **more equal initial distribution may lead to longer-term stability and decentralization.** However, it should be noted that these systems can’t be perfectly mapped to crypto economies. **The key feature that allows real-world economies to combat the centralization of money is equal wage distribution.** Wages in the real world are in themselves a form of continuous wealth redistribution and one that the crypto economies do not necessarily have. 

*Token Distribution in Ethereum*

In a paper published earlier this year, researchers Friedhelm Victor and Bianca Katharina Lüders examined token distributions across Ethereum networks. They looked at the first 6.3 million blocks on the Ethereum blockchain, examining more than 64,000 ERC20 token networks with the goal of understanding the “structure and topology” of individual token networks. They found that, while all tokens across the network are claimed to follow a power-law distribution (where the transfers have a proportional effect across the network), in many individual networks this is not maintained. Instead, these individual networks see a “single hub and spoke pattern.” They further describe most networks as “disassortative,” where the networks do not have many like-actioned individuals clustering. Moreover, they found that the most common action with tokens across the network is to engage in exchanges - but this is second only to never transferring tokens at all, simply holding with no apparent intent to engage (Victor & Lüders).  

The statistics in this are surprising: **less than 10% of token holders across more than 25% of the networks ever transferred their token.** The authors suggest that this could be participants simply holding their tokens, but at such a large scale it could also be that these tokens are unwanted - they even suggest that the tokens are “a type of spam.” They go on to emphasize how few networks show token holders engaging in activity beyond trading their tokens on exchanges. They assert that “the main utility of most tokens is their trade on exchanges,” given that 42% of addresses do not belong to distributors or exchanges (Victor & Lüders).  

Further emphasizing this point, they describe that networks are “mostly dominated by emitting addresses with a large out-degree and exchanges with a large in-degree.” The numbers of both of these types of addresses are limited, following a lack of trust in both exchanges and other users. The researchers assert that “small values for density and clustering coefficient embody the anonymity of those networks, as users don’t typically know each other and tokens aren’t commonly sent to acquaintances” (Victor & Lüders).  

An important caveat to the study is that they assume each address stands for a different entity in the network, when of course one entity can control multiple addresses. The researchers call for further work to address this experiment design flaw and to offer deeper analysis across the entire Ethereum network. “To the best of our knowledge,” they write, “no large-scale study of individual token networks on the Ethereum blockchain has been provided yet” (Victor & Lüders). 

More than anything, their work shows us how little we know about token distribution models across cryptocurrency networks. **With so few token holders ever moving their tokens, it seems clear that initial distribution is a key part of decentralization, given that little movement will lead to little redistribution over time.** However, with such a small subset of users moving their tokens (and these moving their tokens only to engage with exchanges), and with an even smaller subset engaging in other activities, it’s worth asking - should decentralization be a key part of a token network?

This question is intimately linked to the question of [the value of crypto](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/162-how-to-valuing-cryptocurrencies). As Hudson Jameson explained in a Twitter thread with Lane Rettig about the initial token allocation of ETH, it made more sense at the beginning of the network to distribute ETH tokens much more heavily to the founders and developers of the network, given that it was valued at $0.33/coin and nobody imagined it would grow in value so much (Hudson Jameson). But as the next commenter on the post states, now the question is whether Ethereum can “really change the underlining fabric of humanity itself” (padpad). 

In this context, it may be more concerning that so much of the network’s currency is so concentrated in a few hands.  At the same time, if the utility value of network tokens across Ethereum is near zero for the 90% of token holders who are just holding; higher for those to whom the utility of the token lies in its ability to be traded on exchanges; and even higher for those who are using their tokens to engage in other activities (like, say, funding open source development or working to spin up a dApp that will truly deliver value) - in this context, **is it really such a problem for more wealth to be concentrated in the hands of those to whom the token has higher utility value?**

## Back to the Goals of Token Distribution 

This brings us back to the ultimate goals of token distribution. Decentralization, of course, is the easiest goal to jump to, and certainly an important one. But as my ruminations on Ethereum above might suggest, our goals can get more complex - and we can consider cases where decentralization might not be the best thing for network health. 

Without much experimentation and data, however, it’s hard to argue for a case where an altered token distribution set up (which some might simply call a plutocracy) is better than decentralization. And before we dive too deep into goals of token allocation beyond decentralization, I think it’s important to understand first some clear evidence for the necessity of some degree of decentralization. 

**Research across the decades supports the idea that widely equal wealth distribution increases economic health.** Andrew G. Berg and Jonathan D. Ostry of the International Monetary Fund recently published a report stating that “longer growth spells are robustly associated with more equality in the income distribution. According to their estimates, you could potentially more than double the length of a growth spell by closing half the inequality gap between Latin America and emerging markets in Asia (Berg & Ostry). 

The researchers do note that “some inequality is integral to the effective functioning of a market economy and the incentives needed for investment and growth.” But no matter what, income equality is still hugely impactful for economic growth, “even when other determinants of growth duration—external shocks, initial income, institutional quality, openness to trade, and macroeconomic stability—are taken into account” (Berg & Ostry).

Another key point the authors make: “Equality of opportunity can make for both more equal and more efficient outcomes” (Berg & Ostry). This is an idea we can clearly apply to crypto networks. Berg and Ostry focus on both education and healthcare as drivers of equal opportunity in traditional systems. In blockchain-based networks, the issue of different levels of education leading to variant opportunities to participate is evident. Investors, validators, developers, observers, and users with little-to-no technical knowledge will have different education levels on how best to participate in the network. This will lead to different abilities to obtain an amount of stake in the network, and increase centralization. You could translate the healthcare aspect of opportunity to a crypto network, too. Healthcare can be seen as a barrier to entering the system in the first place, just as technical equipment limitations or individual ability to use hardware can limit access to a blockchain-based network. 

The focus on income equality and equal outcomes, however, returns to one of my initial challenges in comparing real-world economies to crypto economies: **there is not necessarily an integrated wealth redistribution mechanism in crypto networks to serve the same purpose as wages do in traditional markets.** Again, though, maybe this points to the importance of having a mechanism for continuous wealth redistribution integrated into crypto networks if we want to see more decentralization and sustainable growth. 

But returning to the Ethereum example, as well as Berg and Ostry’s original caveat that some degree of inequality is necessary, can we ask ourselves again whether or not decentralization should always be the goal? 

In my post on [the goals of token distribution](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/151-what-does-an-ideal-token-distribution-look-like), I referenced the Blockstack system, which gives more tokens to developers, users, and miners that help develop the network.  The Handshake network is another example of a group trying to distribute more tokens to those who contribute to the network, in particular focusing on supporting all developers who contribute to open source work. Projects like SourceCred make the possibility of accurately giving appropriate rewards to these types of contributors much more tangible. 

However, the question of whether methods like this work at scale is still murky. Are there any known cases where plutocratic or more 'unequal' distributions might be a good thing in the long-term? Are these simply proof-of-authority networks or small/young networks? Once we scale up, is decentralization necessary for sustainability? Is there a point in time at which this shift happens - if we think about Bell curves and distribution shapes, when might we want most of the tokens to be held by a median or the inverse? If so, how can we model those? 

These are not questions we have answers to, given the lack of experimentation. However, we can be the network to think about these things, form hypotheses, and test them. Software like Gauntlet offers the opportunity to simulate the outcome of different token allocation mechanisms. We could take these foundational issues and questions, as well as the mechanisms for token distribution that I’ll explore below, create hypotheses from them, and actually **experiment with what novel token distribution mechanisms could do for network health.**

## Mechanisms for Change

In the economic systems driven by centralized authorities that we are used to today, redistribution of wealth typically does come from top-down reform. These reforms might include taxation, public programs, or monetary policies. However, the interaction between individuals and government efforts for wealth redistribution often creates the opposite of the intended effect, according to the Cato Institute. As their report states, government efforts lead to increased inequality, because they don’t support wealth-building. Public programs encourage people to save less, and higher taxes limit saving ability (Edwards & Bourne).

This research suggests that **perhaps the most effective way to target intentional wealth distribution is not through top-down strategies that can have adverse effects, but instead through different token distribution origination structures.** Differently-shaped token bonding curves is an exciting tactic towards creating these different structures.

*Token Bonding Curve Shapes*

Token bonding curves assume a continuous token supply as users grow, and define how increased users will be able to buy and sell tokens over time. The x-axis is the supply of tokens, while the y-axis is the price. In a linear bonding curve, as the network grows, the price to buy or sell the token grows linearly. A Sigmoid curve lets you more heavily reward earlier participants and makes it very difficult to buy in late. Meanwhile, a negative exponential curve lets you still give early participants a higher reward, but the price levels out so as not to de-incentivize late buy-in. You can also use a quadratic curve, which functions similarly to the Sigmoid curve and is the original token bonding curve shape behind [Curation Markets](https://commonwealth.im/edgeware/proposal/discussion/142-tests-and-opportunities-for-curation-markets) (Coutts). 

This is the most basic dive into what token bonding curves can look like. They should ideally be customized to the goals and structure of each individual network - which makes all the thought work above absolutely crucial for choosing a shape. It should be noted that the token bonding curve, too, only considers the time that a user joins, which is not necessarily a robust signal for the type of user - meaning that **hypotheses about token distribution models should probably include not just the origination structure offered by a bonding curve, but also further consideration of other mechanisms**, like taxation or additional rewards (despite the caveats I explained above). 

*A Conviction Voting Token Allocation Integration*

There hasn’t been much research on what these alternative mechanisms could look like for cryptocurrency networks. One model that could ground a continuous wealth redistribution is the idea of Conviction Voting, pioneered by Commons Stack. With Conviction Voting, every proposal that is made is continuously voted on with longer-kept votes for a proposal gaining weight in the system (Emmet). If votes and time-voted-for on proposals could somehow be linked to a rewards system, this could be leveraged to distribute tokens to any member of the community offering value, as determined by the entire community. Voting members could receive incentives to vote, as well, in order to ensure that votes are valuable and wealth is moving around the entire community. 

The Commons Stack Conviction Voting (CV) method is a more accurate way to judge what actually is valuable, given that the continuous decision-making framework requires constant re-evaluation. Moreover, longer-term participants who have more experience to leverage are able to throw their weight with more heft. These aspects would allow a CV-based Continuous Token Allocation model better reward those who contribute more to the network, no matter what sort of stakeholder they are. This, along with the base level fact that it is a continuous model, could allow a CV-based Continuous Token Allocation face the challenge of achieving decentralization while still targeting network goals. 

## Application 

Edgeware presents us with an exceptionally curious case on how to assess and apply all the information above. The point of this network is not traditional profit maximization. Rather, the goal of the network is to experiment, and by doing so produce value - an abstract idea that will be determined by the community. In many ways, Edgeware *does* have the ability to truly change the underlying fabric of humanity, in that this is a novel chance to experiment with what a network of people could create with pioneering consensus and building mechanisms.

But with this definition of value, how can we determine the ideal token distribution? Should the goal be to maximize development, and therefore should we integrate a mechanism that gives heftier block rewards to developers who contribute to the network? While developers are certainly important, I think we have the potential to make a more elaborate system than that. This is something we have the opportunity to formulate and test hypotheses on, and I think it’s crucial that we go beyond simply allocating extra rewards to one group, or even just integrating pure decentralization mechanisms. 

Crypto networks are complex, and as such it is our duty to think critically about how token allocation mechanisms, both origination structures and top-down processes, can be linked to various outcomes and goals that might support long-term network health. Each part will be determined by the people who show up - that’s the nature of communal decision-making. 

Some further questions:

- What will determine the value of the EDG token? 
- What should the goals of EDG distribution be? Who do we want to have more or less tokens? How should this change over time?
- What mechanisms should be used to test whether or not we can implement these goals? Should we focus on token bonding curve shapes? A way to implement a Conviction Voting-based Continuous Token Allocation model? Another mechanism?
