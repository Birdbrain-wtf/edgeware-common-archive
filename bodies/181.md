**How Do Prediction Markets Work?**

Prediction markets have been an academic subject for decades. They’ve long been used in the corporate world, and have seen popularity in the crypto ecosystem with platforms like Augur or governance mechanisms like holographic consensus. In prediction markets like those on Augur, you can bet on anything – even something as mundane or fluid as the weather. These markets let participants bet on whether or not they think a proposed outcome will come true. 

For instance, if somebody proposed that it would snow in San Francisco tomorrow, you could run a prediction market where participants try their best to research and guess at the correct answer. The fact that participants have money in the game adds a level of commitment to forecasting correctly. The odds are crowdsourced, and the results are shockingly accurate. 

**Are Prediction Markets Legal?**

However, there’s an obvious question to ask about prediction markets – how are they legal? It sounds a lot like gambling, so does the company running the prediction market have to have a gambling license? 

The short answer is, yes, it’s legal, and no, it’s not gambling – but there’s a lot of grey area in that answer. While prediction markets _can_ be used for illegal gambling, the foundational idea of a prediction market is not illegal. The goals are different than simple profit maximization and the outcomes become more predictable as the collective contributes to knowledge on the bet (Pauw). But it’s a thin, wobbly, unclear line between the two, as is clear from current regulation on prediction markets in the US.

_Binary Options and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission_

As Drew Millard from Breaker Mag has reported, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) allows trading of virtual currency futures contracts. There are developers, like Ben Goldhaber of Parallel Forecast, who believe that putting a prediction market on the blockchain is a fail-safe way to escape regulation, because of the transparency offered (Millard). On the other hand, the premise of the market could get into tricky territory with the CFTC if it includes exchanges offering binary trades. As a Bloomberg article describes, “creating a way to bet on the value of goods in the future is basically the definition of a type of derivative known as a binary option,” and participating in this sort of exchange requires approval by the CFTC (Millard). While Augur is definitely in this grey area, markets like Iowa Electronic Markets, which is run by the University of Iowa, and PredictIt, run by Victoria University of Wellington, work around this with no-action letters from the CFTC (McGonagle).

_The Advent of Assassination Markets_

Another common issue that could relate to the legality of the prediction market system is the type of betting it invites. For instance, Augur hosts several “assassination markets,” which are exactly what they sound like – betting markets for if and when public figures will be killed. Something distasteful like this isn’t controlled by the founders of Augur, but by the users. Though in theory, the market idea is legal, it could get into trouble should a world leader actually be assassinated due to monetary incentive from the market (which is unlikely, given that participation in these markets is generally very low, but still – it’s a fascinating legal hypothetical).

_The Issue of Free Speech_

An interesting approach to this issue of legality is whether or not code for a market like Augur could be protected in the US by freedom of speech laws. As of a 2000 resolution, the US holds that code is a form of expression used to convey ideas, and as such is protected under the First Amendment (Rigano). This protection, however, can be limited by the use of the “speech” – so using a prediction market to facilitate unfair hedging or cause an assassination could still be illegal.
