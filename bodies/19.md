DAOs (Decentralized autonomous organizations) have been written about extensively. [1] In short, they are blockchain-based organization conducted through distributed governance. While the form is still evolving, Edgeware can be an ideal place to experiment. As a start, we'd like to recreate existing experiments that seem to have gone well:

- Share-based MolochDAO / MetaCartel style DAOs
- Advanced bonding curve DAOs (such as is built by Commonstack.)

Once those primitives have been built, we can move on to building ones with different voting mechanisms. It would be interesting to see this be funded by the treasury. Based on the discussion here, We'll try to create some signals and treasury proposals to fund development.

Hopefully, DAOs have several other properties:

- Scalability: Whether DAOs are composed of three members or 3 million members, decision making should scale up or down—(from 10 EDG to 10 billion EDG!)
- Resiliency: The ability for a DAO to achieve relevant, accurate and time-sensitive, perhaps even speedy, representation of the participant's interests.
- Transparency: Raw, unfiltered, and accessible information about the total of actions and positions of the DAO.

It remains to be seen how well these experiments are able to meet other criteria for DAOs. There have already been examples of vote buying[2] and slow decision-making show that DAOs still do not have all the desired traits above.

They have several common features:

- Some unit of account of voting power, or even several. These may include shareholder-style governance, or measures of reputation (sometimes characterized as an aggregate of recent contribution, which decays over time.)

- Identity, which is often pseudonymous or anonymous.

- A Legislative process, that is, proposal-making systems.

- An open or closed but authenticating process for individuals to become participating members. This may be as easy as acquiring a governance token, or making a forum account, or showing up to a meetup, call, or other discussion format.

- Some incentive to participate in decision-making. In traditional governments, there can be compulsory voting, where criminal or financial penalties accrue from failure to participate. DAOs frequently move to more purely volitional and less coercive incentive structures to obtain 'attention.' These may take the form of voter rebates, paid incentives to vote, formal reputation-related benefits, enhanced legislative powers beyond simple signals or referenda participation- or maybe the prevailing culture awards the politically active with simple social status (a decently fungible resource common in traditional societies!)

- Application-based interfaces for interacting with the DAO, commonly called Dapps. Ideally these are platform-agnostic such that the barrier for participation is minimal, conversely, that accessibility is maximal.

- A way of managing the pipeline of requests on participants attention, which may be filtered by topic (and topic expertise, in delegated votes or liquid democracy-type situations), by a time window (for example, a 3-month period where proposals are drafted, reviewed, and then voted on,) or by the stake of their proposers or proponents - where users might examine only those proposals with the most existing backing or signaled interest.

- A depository of money or resources, including property to be coordinated by the collective decisions by the DAO. In blockchain-speak, this often means a wallet or multi-signature wallet.

- The ability to create ownership in users - often through a governance token (Think MKR or EDG,) but not necessarily equal ownership across all users. Some DAOs have tiers of power structure: Elected roles like councils may vest certain entities with greater relative voice.

- Communications channels, a shared repository of processes, documentation and discussion.

- Iterated values that describe the norms of communication: meta-level discourse.

- A basis in a mission that allows it to optimize process and praxis according to set and consistent goals while motivating members to problem-solve according to their relationship with that mission.

- They may also have dispute resolution processes - EOS is a popular example of a 'court' like DR system.

Best practices have been difficult to gather due to the early stage of this technology and their various use cases, according to surveys conducted by the DGov Foundation, but this list of standard DAO modules and functions will likely grow to be use-case specific, which the DGov foundation found to be the most satisfactory design specifications for their users.
