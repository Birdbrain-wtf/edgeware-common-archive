Something that will soon become a challenge is: **how do you value a contribution? **

This ties to another area which is how to set 'fair' rates across a project in terms of the various roles, responsibilities and expected rates (if indeed rates are to be set). 

This also links to how proposals are budgeted, how costs are tracked and how value for money is demonstrated with clear deliverables. 

This is not something that will be solved immediately, but should be front of mind for everyone requesting funds from the treasury and for those managing the disbursement of those funds. 

**Why develop rate cards?**

If there is no consistency with how and why people are paid and against what deliverables, trust and transparency will be impossible to build. 

Historically companies withheld the amount they paid to different team members, even if they were doing the same jobs or had very similar roles. 

Over time this lack of transparency inevitably leads to internal cultural conflict (people talk) and over time tends to lead to a small cohort controlling the 'pricing' of labour or expenses and tending to extract increasing value. 

The introduction of auditable blockchain treasuries and proposal systems offers a chance to reset this pattern and to consider how best to transparently reward and maintain a decentralised workforce, where long term community prosperity (economic and cultural) is paramount. 

**Why are rate cards useful? **

In many industries rate-cards exist to standardise the range of approved costs for certain roles or for specific work. 

Rate cards can represent individuals or groups (collectives / corporates) and give a way to bring transparency to the value assigned to work done, helping those costing work and those auditing those costs. 

Rate cards might outline costs: 
- Hourly rate (many developers / designers bill this way)
- Daily rate (this is standard broadly across many forms of project management)
- Weekly rate (makes sense for more consistent roles)
- Salary (generally understood to be permanent contracts).


Over time, developing a rate card can aid transparency and also mediate expectations from those who work with/for Edgeware and bill to the treasury. 

The long term aim should be to develop standards that help those costing proposals and tracking expenditure for Edgeware's treasury and the value the work creates - many of these roles should be automated. 

**Where don't rate cards help? **

With decentralised communities, the nature of work is changing and valuing contributions can be hard especially in the early stages, since there are many factors at play and standardising all costs can be detrimental when trying to attract the best talent. 

Providing a single rate for a developer or graphic designer does not account for variablities in their talent, experience or work to date. 

As a result rate cards should be developed as **a guide, not a formula. **
