games have been something that I have always gravitated towards, namely for their ability in generating fairness or exploiting fairness toward strategic entertainment. The games I look at below shift from being just ‘fairness’ generators and strategic games, depending on the existence of a ‘wager’, its size, or iterations of gameplay. In recognizing these metrics in their ability to shift the relationship of fairness and strategy, games can be harnessed toward both entertainment and productive ends. 

What’s more, gas fees on Ethereum are currently prohibitively expensive. DeFi use cases are consistently the highest bidder for blockspace. Edgeware has an interesting opportunity to leverage it’s cheap and plentiful blockspace to explore higher volume, lower value transactions. Games of probability and volume are fantastic as they create external value from the sum of participation by testing out the (ir)rational mind of a population. 

random data points about games in crypto: 
- ~2010, there were quite a few poker site start ups unable to get gaming/banking regulation, moved their servers off-shore and allowed no-limit bitcoin poker. This got a lot of people from the cash and gaming economy to take notice of bitcoin relatively early for its censorship-resistance. These IRL bitcoin poker networks are still active, predominantly in vegas where bitcoin/usd trades occur with the use of poker chips as temporary fungible collateral. 
- Erik voorhees’ first business in the space was known as “satoshi dice”, a dice game that used bitcoin to generate the randomness for the game. in the spring of 2012, it famously sold for ~125,000BTC. 
- One of the first internal projects at consensys was “virtue poker”, though regulation was an issue for a long time 
- Fomo3D, a game designed with interesting game theory mechanisms got a bunch of attention and engagement. 
- While not a “game” by definition, crypto-kitties garnered game-like attention. Social signalling/gaming via the ownership of a limited + algorithmically specific good. “status game” 
- Bitcoin’s POW is a fair game played to order blocks and issue currency. 

simple novel games seem to gain traction with all users of a system (its value): 
- low barrier to entry 
- mid/low risk for each engagement 
- engagement signifies a usefulness/usability to the network more generally, 
 - - leads to new kinds of stress tests on a network made of organic and inorganic activity alike, as ‘hackers’ automate strategy of the novel game ultimately revealing some sort of undiagnosed or under-theorized vulnerability (in network at large or in contract/script design, both useful for developing case studies for future developers and best-practices).
-  “check this out” (easy to share, easy to peruse) is a “product” 

a few novel games i find interesting: 
- “2/3 the average” 
- Luring Lottery (and other lotteries more generally) 
- Kuhn poker (and (non-)zero sum poker games)
-  “dollar auction” (second highest bid also pays, no ‘reward’/win) 
 - - Similarly, reverse dutch auctions are an interesting method for fair valuation of goods. 
- rock paper scissors (i’ve not seen done using transactions and cryptography against unknown player, but i’d love to formalize a blind RPS using encrypted messages unencrypted when met with a match) 

most of the games are nash equilibrium variants (mixed strategy), not sure if that’s an important thing to note? 

games can be used to introduce randomness in situations where variability is desirable (no one king, many temporary kings; random redistribution of temporary voting power) 

- “dollar auction” to set a priority queue for governance/issue ordering, funds go to a public goods treasury. not random, but allows for the growth of treasury of an organization via prioritization.
-  random selection N of M for delegates could easily be done via a game like rock/paper/scissors when sufficient randomness is impossible to generate on-chain. Ordering of goods could be done here too, or the ordering of proposals when voting, or the order of talking points, or...

Adjacent concept: [https://democracyrd.org/work/ Democracy R&D](https://democracyrd.org/work/ Democracy R&D) has an interesting approach to their temporary organizations (all democratically held positions are held temporarily by definition): 
- All participants are randomly selected from a pool a people who *want* to participate, which eliminates inherent biases in most election processes, ie all things “popularity contest”-like. Variations (like some sort of minimum threshold for participation) are valid. 
- Try to remove asymmetric information related to the issues being decided on within the elected group; no hierarchy and full disclosure of relevant knowledge/facts needed to make an informed decision. 
- Non-voting facilitator guides all deliberations. 
Thinking about how delegated voting can be made in systems like this changes the fairness or introduces new biases.
