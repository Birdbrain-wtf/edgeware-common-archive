At Berlin Blockchain Week '19 last month, Commonwealth’s Chief-of-Staff Thom Ivy sat down with Gavin Wood for a chat on governance in the blockchain space. The discussion began with the subject of tribalism, a topic well-known to many in this community. Tribalism is listed as one of the “ten dark secrets of crypto” according to an article at Hackernoon [1], and you can even download a browser extension built to *identify* tribalism when it appears subtly on Twitter, called “Coinflict of Interest.” [2] As Gavin iterated in his chat with Thom, tribalism is problematic, and it needs to stop - but ending that tribalism is no simple task.

The most common and extreme case of tribalism is that of Bitcoin maximalism. Bitcoin maximalists don’t *just* think Bitcoin is a superior coin-they believe that no other coin should exist - or nearly any other use of blockchain technology, for that matter, and if it should, Bitcoin will eventually do it. We saw this argument play out between Ethereum and Polkadot more recently: "Eth2 will solve that use case," is a trope, noted 1Confirmation. [3]

This maximalist belief is often acted out in fairly distasteful ways. Last week, noted Bitcoin maximalist Matt Odell started posting on Twitter calling for donations of altcoins to the TOR project, writing “donate shitcoins. stack sats.” [4] Many Bitcoin maximalists jumped at the chance to use donations to the project as a way to dump their "shitcoins" - and took every opportunity to make it clear that their donation was not about TOR, but instead was strictly for the purpose of getting rid of the altcoins that they think of as worthless.

Tribalism generally takes the form of talking up one’s own network or attacking a different network. The attacks can get personal, and they can get really nasty. The problem isn't limited to Bitcoin holders; Ethereum Maximalists are starting to emerge, as admitted even by one of Ethereum’s co-founders, Joseph Lubin, now founder of Consensys. This is a difficult problem to solve, as there isn’t one clear source but rather a few variables interacting complexly. One cause is simply the space that discussions about blockchain tech take place in: Twitter and Subreddits. These are arenas that are already conducive to personal, emotional content rather than rational, fact-based debate. This makes it easier to make personal attacks, and it also allows for a feeling of social bonding with those in your own network as you do so.

Perhaps the most obvious cause of tribalism is the economic investment. Financial incentive to participate in a network is important, in that it encourages participation and drives innovation in that way. But it becomes an issue when members of a network develop the sense that others could be a threat to their own financial safety. 

This sentiment relies on the idea that cryptocurrency, and blockchain tech generally, is a zero-sum game. That perspective ignores the clear evidence that as the entire market has grown, it has largely benefited each player. It also doesn’t acknowledge that one of the most amazing things in the blockchain community is the room for collaboration. As Tony Sheng described in a 2018 article, “as a community interested in a freer world, we should think hard about whether the in-fighting increases or decreases the probability of any crypto project’s mainstream success.” [5] Or as Joseph Lubin put it at the Ethereal Summit in Tel Aviv on Sunday, “we are all on the same team.” [6] 

In Berlin, Gavin emphasized the significant role internal policing could have in alleviating this problem. If community members are willing to publicly call out those who engage in personal attacks or aggressive tribalist behaviors, it can shift the culture and create new norms of conduct. He suggested introducing written standards of conduct as well, something we could consider introducing to Commonwealth.im. This idea of a standard of conduct is not uncommon - Gavin referred to the fact that many open-source projects have a code of conduct for developers, as well as Parity’s Substrate. 

By using on-chain governance to implement these kinds of changes, the community will be able to take full autonomy of their culture. As Gavin emphasized while chatting with Thom, one of the long-term advantages of on-chain governance is the ability to use the collective intelligence of a network to drive decision-making. Instead of letting the minority aggressive voices drown out the quiet majority, the chain has the power to choose better standards. On-chain governance has the power to change the blockchain narrative from one that is riddled with tribalism, to one that is focused on collaboration and community.
