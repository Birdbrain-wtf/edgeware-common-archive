**The Problem of Dark DAOs**

Philip Daian and his team’s paper “On-Chain Vote Buying and the DAO Dark Ages” set off a string of reactionary pieces, including his own follow up post. In the original article, the authors explicated an attack infrastructure that showed that any network that uses on-chain voting is susceptible to vote-buying, and argued that smart contracts, trusted hardware, and anonymity would allow for the creation of Dark DAOs: groups that specifically engage in vote-buying for some (usually financial) gain. 

According to the paper, not only does the structure of on-chain governance systems allow for vote-buying, but there are several elements that encourage it. First, while anyone who holds coins theoretically sees overall network utility as an ultimate goal, factors like the possibility for more and/or earlier financial gain could incentivize vote-selling anyways - especially if participants are able to easily leave a network. Daian and company also note that Oracles (like Town Crier, multiple oracles, or prediction markets) can be used to simplify the vote-buying process by mapping real-world information and supplying it to chains (Daian et al., 2018).

In Daian’s personal followup article on the topic, he describes how quadratic voting increases the probability of vote-buying and Dark DAO attacks. Quadratic voting is meant to decrease the possibility for plutocracy by implementing a system where the cost of votes increases exponentially. However, Daian makes the point that if the system depends on user-generated key-based identities, then a wealthy individual could simply split their identity and leverage their wealth to sway the vote. 

If the identity system required real-world identities attached to every key, Daian contends that the vote could still easily be swayed by targeting lower-income voters, creating an “economic Sybil attack.” And even given the constraint that with quadratic voting, you would potentially need a large amount of counter votes to sway the vote, Daian points out that the ease of creating a Dark DAO makes this sort of wide-spread vote-buying very possible (Daian, 2018).

The conclusion to both of these articles emphasizes that self-evolving systems (ie, networks that rely on on-chain governance) are uniquely susceptible to Dark DAO attacks, because votes can alter system operations in ways that facilitate the will of the attacker. Whether or not a Dark DAO could successfully alter on-chain governance to change network operations at will is not such a cut and dry issue - in most cases, it probably depends a lot on the structure of governance. Most basically, if coins and power are more widely distributed, there is probably a high likelihood for vote-buying, but if a smaller group has more power, then you run the risk of seeing an oligarchy form. 

Whether a Dark DAO *should* be able to engage in vote-buying is another contentious issue. An interesting conversation around this question revolves around whether blockchains should be considered public goods or private goods. 

If blockchains are seen as private goods, then vote-buying, even by a Dark DAO, and the anonymous plutocracy shouldn’t matter. But if we view the chain as a public good, then a clear argument against plutocracy emerges. As has been noted by the Mosaic research team, this further shifts the discussion from one about immorality and unjust-ness, to one about sustainability (Ige, 2018). Whether or not we think of the network as public or private, would allowing a Dark DAO to shift the system in its favor ever allow for the sustainable development of a network?

The EOS blockchain is a good example of a network where vote-buying has allowed one small, powerful group to control system governance - and most would argue that this set-up has driven development into the ground. The case of EOS could show some of the potential ramifications of a Dark DAO with control of network operations. (Check out [this post on vote-buying and power distribution in EOS] (https://commonwealth.im/#!/edgeware/proposal/discussion/90)  for some more background). 

**Exploring Possible Solutions**

Vitalik Buterin has recently offered a “minimal anti-collusion infrastructure” to deal with the issue of bribery. In this proposal, Buterin assumes a registry that notes user’s public keys, with the requirements that a legitimate identity with personal access is attached to the key. Users are also required to put down a deposit, to de-incentivize sharing their private key with a third party. There is also an operator, and a mechanism (essentially, something that catalyzes an action and creates an output). During a specific time period, messages can be published to tan on-chain registry, encrypted with their private key. The operator’s job is to process each message, decrypting and executing the action in each one. The actions can be to perform an action (like a vote), or to change their key (essentially allowing them to cast a second vote). After the specified time, the operator publishes the output and a ZK-SNARK that proves that the output is the correct result of the process (Buterin, 2019). 

Theoretically, a participant could use a zero-knowledge proof to show they took an action. However, this wouldn’t prove that they didn’t take an earlier action to go against that action. Essentially, no user here can buy a vote from another user, because they cannot trust that the user did not change their vote later on in the process. 

Buterin iterates that this solution does not solve every issue -- by using trusted hardware, you could still get around his fix. But this search for a solution to the issue of anonymity and vote-buying begs an important question: why do we need total privacy for these sorts of transactions? 

**The Argument For (and Against) Privacy**

Technology, as created, is neutral. A mechanism for the creation of a Dark DAO is not malicious until individuals use it for nefarious purposes. Moreover, a Dark DAO is not necessarily a malignant thing. In fact, the argument that already exists for privacy coins and the widening spread of zero-knowledge proof implementations is one that most people might have a hard time arguing against at first: the freedom of anonymity. 

Say you were a big oil mogul, and you were beholden to your company and stakeholders to drive the need for oil in the world. But maybe one day, you had a change of heart - and you want to donate 80% of your private funds to some anti-pipeline environmental effort (this is not a very sound real-world example, but the point is in the moral of the story, not the specific framework - so bear with me). You wouldn’t want anyone to be able to link together all of your financial actions, piecing together the fact that you are in fact, said oil mogul, and have committed a traitorous act that will damage the financial standing of your company and your stakeholders. 

The lack of true anonymity here might make you choose not to donate to the anti-oil group at all (and for the sake of this example, let’s assume you’re on the side of the bees and the birds and that this represents the loss of a positive action). The lack of privacy, then, limits your ability to be a force for good. This is the crux of the freedom of privacy argument: true anonymity allows for much greater freedom of action. 

But when it comes down to it, the probability that a Dark DAO would be created to contribute to positive actions is less likely than the use of the technology for bad. The use of the Dark DAO’s anonymity to bribe politicians, influence campaign finance laws, facilitate scams and schemes of financial trickery, or other malevolent purposes, feels a lot more probable. 

Daian’s original paper notes several of these sorts of possibilities for types of Dark DAOs: one that would deliver a user’s basic income payments (and allow parties with more time-sensitive financial needs to buy a portion of the user’s income), or one that would allow users to lease keys for key-based identities with good credit in order to get through credit checks (Daian et al., 2018). 

In the end, this conversation probably doesn’t stop at privacy vs. freedom. As Buterin’s early work suggests, there are steps that can be taken to create technology to minimize the perceived choice between anonymity and collusion. In the meantime, however, the vote-buying cartels suggested by Daian and his team are probably already operating. Especially given the impact these sorts of groups could have on the structure of an on-chain governed community like Edgeware, it’s important to consider what defenses we might create against them.
